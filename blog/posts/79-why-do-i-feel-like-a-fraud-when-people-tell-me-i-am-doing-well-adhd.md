# Why do I feel like a fraud when people tell me I am doing well?

> **Quick answer:** Because you know what the result cost and they only see the result. You are comparing your own effort against their impression, and those will never match. The gap is real rather than imagined, which is why reassurance does not fix it and evidence does.


Someone compliments your work and the first thing that arrives is not pleasure. It is the thought that they would not say that if they knew how it actually got done.

This is unusually common among the people we work with, and it is strongest in the ones doing best on paper.

## Why does praise land badly?

Because you have information they do not.

They see a delivered project. You saw three weeks of avoidance, two nights of panic, and a thing finished at the last possible hour. Both of those are true, and only one of them is visible.

So the compliment feels like it was given to a version of you that does not exist. It was not. It was given to work that was genuinely good. The two things simply came at a cost nobody else can see.

## Why doesn't the evidence help?

It does, but not the evidence people offer you.

Other people's opinions do not change your internal account, because you discount them as not having the full picture. That is why reassurance from a partner or a manager bounces off.

What does change it is your own record. Most people in this position can list everything they have not done and cannot list what they have, because unfinished work stays visible and finished work disappears the moment it is done.

That is where the Proof List comes in. Things you finished, written down as they happen, where you can see them. Read it on the days this feeling arrives. People are usually surprised by their own list, and the size of that surprise tells you how wrong the internal version was.

## Is this imposter syndrome?

It overlaps, with one difference worth knowing.

The usual description of imposter syndrome is a distorted belief about your ability. What is often happening here is not a distortion. The effort really was disproportionate, the process really was chaotic, and you are accurately reporting that.

The error is in the conclusion. Difficult does not mean fraudulent. A result that took more out of you than it would take someone else is still a result.

| What you see | What they see |
|---|---|
| Three weeks of not starting | A project delivered |
| Panic at the end | Work done to standard |
| Help you needed | Competence |
| How close it came to not happening | It happened |

## What actually helps

**Keep the Proof List and read it on bad days.** Not as a mood exercise. As evidence.

**Tell someone when you finish things.** Completion often produces very little internal reward with ADHD, so the signal has to come from outside.

**Separate the process from the outcome.** You are allowed to think your process needs work and your work is good. Most people in this position collapse the two.

**Stop benchmarking against your best week.** The week you were hyperfocused and producing at four times normal rate was not sustainable and is not your baseline.

## FAQ

**Does this go away as you get more experienced?**
Not automatically. Several of our clients are very senior and still describe it. What changes it is a visible record rather than more success.

**Should I tell people how hard it was?**
With people close to you, sometimes. At work, usually not, and rarely in the moment someone is praising you.

**Why do I feel worse after a win?**
Because the win raises the expectation while you know the process has not changed. That is worth addressing directly rather than waiting for it to resolve.

**Is this depression?**
It can occur alongside it. If very little feels rewarding, not just your own achievements, speak to your doctor.
