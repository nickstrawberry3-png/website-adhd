# Why do I crash for days after hitting a big deadline?

> **Quick answer:** Because the deadline did not make you productive, it made you override. Working at that intensity borrows energy you did not have, and the crash is the repayment. It is predictable, it gets worse the more often you do it, and it is the second step of the Burnout Loop.


The pattern is familiar to almost everyone we work with. Nothing happens for weeks. Then the deadline gets close, something switches on, and we produce an extraordinary amount of work in a very short time.

Then we lose the following days, sometimes the following weeks. And the strange part is there is no satisfaction in it. One client said there was no relief at the end, no feeling of having achieved anything. Just flat.

## Why does the deadline work when nothing else does?

Because urgency is one of the few things that reliably starts an ADHD brain. The closer the consequence, the stronger the pull.

That is why we end up relying on it. It is the only mechanism that has never failed us.

The cost is hidden until later. Working under that kind of pressure is not normal working. It involves skipped sleep, skipped meals, and sustained stress, and the body charges for all three.

## Why is the crash worse than it used to be?

Because it compounds. Each cycle starts from a slightly lower baseline than the last.

A freelance videographer we spoke to described the end state. He had spent years pushing through without a diagnosis, hitting deadlines at the last possible minute, and eventually reached a point where he could not start anything at all. He described his engine as having been in the red for years before it stopped.

That is the Burnout Loop reaching its end. Push hard with the wrong systems, crash, feel exhausted and guilty, start over. Each round makes the next one harder.

## Is the crash avoidable?

Partly, and the honest answer is that most people cannot skip straight to working without pressure. The goal is to stop making the pressure the only thing that ever works.

What actually helps:

**Plan the recovery before the deadline, not after.** Block the two days after a big delivery and do not fill them. If they stay empty you recover. If you fill them, the crash lands anyway and takes something with it.

**Make the deadline earlier.** Not a fake deadline you know is fake. A real one you have told someone about, several days before the actual one.

**Build smaller pressure points.** Weekly check-ins with a real person beat one enormous deadline. The pull is smaller and so is the crash.

**Protect sleep hardest in the final week.** That is the week it goes first and the week it matters most.

## What does recovery actually look like?

Not a holiday. Specifically:

| Instead of | Do |
|---|---|
| Catching up on everything you dropped | One small task a day, for two days |
| Sleeping twelve hours one night | A fixed wake time for the whole week |
| Starting the next big thing immediately | Nothing new for forty-eight hours |
| Feeling guilty about the flat days | Treating them as the predictable cost |

The guilt is worth naming, because for most people it is the part that turns a two-day crash into a two-week one.

## FAQ

**Is this ADHD burnout?**
It is a short version of it. Repeated often enough, it becomes the long version, where the ability to start anything disappears for months.

**Why do I feel nothing after finishing something big?**
Common with ADHD. The reward often does not arrive on completion, which is part of why finishing is unsatisfying and starting is hard.

**Should I stop using deadlines then?**
No. Deadlines work. The problem is one enormous deadline with nothing in between. Several small ones produce the same pull without the same crash.

**How long should recovery take?**
If it regularly takes more than a few days, the cycle is costing more than the work is worth, and it is worth looking at properly.
