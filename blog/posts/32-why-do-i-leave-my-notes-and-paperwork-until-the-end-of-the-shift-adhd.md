# Why do I leave my notes and paperwork until the end of the shift?

> **Quick answer:** Because the patient in front of you supplies urgency and the notes do not. Clinical work has a person waiting, immediate feedback and constant variety. Documentation has none of those, so it loses every time, and the backlog lands at the hour you have least left.


Almost every clinician we work with describes the same day. The clinical work goes fine. Patients are seen, decisions are made, nothing is missed. Then the shift ends and there are eleven sets of notes to write, and an hour of unpaid time disappears.

This is not a knowledge problem. Nobody needs telling that notes matter.

## Why do notes lose to everything else?

Compare what the two tasks offer.

| | Seeing a patient | Writing it up |
|---|---|---|
| Someone waiting | Yes, in front of you | No |
| Urgency | Immediate | Only at the end |
| Variety | Every one different | Identical structure |
| Feedback | Instant | None |
| Interest | High | None |

Clinical work arrives with every trigger an ADHD brain responds to. Documentation arrives with none. So the choice between them is not really a choice, and willpower has very little to do with it.

There is usually a second layer too. Once notes are a few hours behind, opening them means facing the whole pile, so we avoid the pile rather than the writing.

## What actually works?

**Write in the room, not after it.** The single biggest change. Even two lines at the time beats a full entry from memory three hours later, and the recall cost is the expensive part.

**Use a fixed skeleton.** The same structure every time, so there is no blank page. A blank page is a decision, and decisions are what stall.

**Make Step Zero opening the record, not writing the entry.** Open it. Type the first line. That is a complete action. Starting is what you are avoiding, not writing.

**Batch what cannot be done live.** If live documentation genuinely is not possible, put two fixed points in the shift rather than one pile at the end. The start cost is paid twice instead of once, and nothing accumulates to the point of dread.

**Do the hardest entry first, not last.** The complicated patient written at the end of a night shift is the one most likely to be thin, and the one most likely to matter later.

## Why does this get worse over a run of shifts?

Because the backlog carries forward and the guilt compounds. By day four of a run, opening the system means seeing everything from days one to three as well.

That is also when the risk rises. Notes written late and from memory are worse notes, and the people we work with know this, which is part of why they dread opening them.

## What if my system is genuinely slow?

Then say so, specifically and in writing, to whoever can change it. Clinicians often treat a badly designed record system as a personal failing. It is not, and a documented complaint about click counts is more useful than another attempt to be faster.

That said, do not wait for the system to improve. The live writing habit helps on a bad system too.

## FAQ

**Is it normal to stay late every shift doing notes?**
Common, and it should not be the plan. If it is happening on most shifts, the documentation is being deferred rather than being genuinely too long.

**How do I write notes faster?**
Mostly you do not. You write them earlier. Speed is a smaller lever than timing.

**What if I cannot remember the detail later?**
That is the strongest argument for writing in the room. Recall degrades fast and the entry written three hours later is both slower to write and less accurate.

**Should I use templates or voice dictation?**
Templates remove the blank page, which helps a lot. Dictation suits some people and not others, worth testing on a few entries before committing.
