# What study timetable actually works for ADHD?

> **Quick answer:** The simplest one you will still be using in week six. One fixed daily block with a floor you can hit on a bad day, a topic chosen the night before, and a weekly review to adjust. Detailed hour-by-hour timetables fail because they assume consistent motivation, which is the one thing you do not have.


Almost everyone studying for something big has built a timetable. Colour coded, every topic allocated, the whole period covered. It usually takes an evening and feels excellent.

It also usually breaks within two weeks, and then it stops being a plan and becomes a reminder of failure.

## Why do detailed timetables fail?

**They assume every day is the same.** One bad shift, one late night, one illness, and the schedule is wrong for every subsequent day.

**They are all or nothing.** Miss two sessions and the plan no longer matches reality, so the sensible-feeling move is to rebuild it, which takes another evening and produces another fragile plan.

**Building one is itself a great ADHD task.** Novel, visual, clear first step, immediate result. That is why it is so satisfying and why it so often replaces studying.

**They have no floor.** A plan that says ninety minutes offers no option on the day you have twenty.

## What a working plan looks like

Four parts, and it fits on one page.

**One daily block, fixed time.** Attached to something that already happens. Twenty to forty minutes on a working day.

**A floor.** The minimum that still counts. Ten questions. Fifteen minutes. Something you would genuinely do after a terrible day. The floor is what keeps the run alive.

**Tomorrow's topic, decided tonight.** One line. Removes the morning decision, which is where sessions die.

**A weekly review, fifteen minutes.** What got covered, what is weakest, what next week's focus is. This replaces the four-month timetable entirely, because you only ever plan a week.

| | Detailed timetable | What works |
|---|---|---|
| Planning horizon | The whole period | One week |
| Daily target | A specific number of hours | One block, with a floor |
| If you miss a day | The plan is wrong, rebuild it | Do the next one |
| Topic chosen | Months in advance | The night before |
| Time spent planning | An evening | Fifteen minutes a week |

## How do I know if I am covering enough?

Track what you have done, not what you planned. A simple list of topics covered and dates, added to as you go.

This matters for two reasons. It tells you honestly what is left, which a timetable does not once it has drifted. And it is visible evidence of work done, which people in this position badly underestimate.

At the weekly review, compare the list against the syllabus and pick next week's focus from the gaps. That is the whole planning process.

## What about the weeks before the exam?

The structure stays the same and the floor goes up. Add a second block rather than lengthening the first, because the first one is already at the edge of your attention.

Switch almost entirely to past papers and questions under time pressure in the final stretch. That is the one point where the volume genuinely matters more than the habit, and the habit should be established by then to carry it.

## FAQ

**How many hours a week should I study?**
Less relevant than how many days. Five short sessions beat two long ones for both retention and actually happening.

**Should I plan around topics or around time?**
Topics, with a time cap. One topic per session, stop at the cap even if unfinished, carry it to the next session.

**What if my shifts change every week?**
Anchor the block to the shift rather than the clock. Before the shift, or straight after getting home. Same relationship, different time.

**Is it worth using a study app?**
Only if it reduces friction. If you are configuring it more than using it, it has become the timetable problem again.
