# Should I tell my team or my clients I have ADHD?

> **Quick answer:** Usually no for clients, sometimes yes for a manager, and it depends heavily on the person rather than the policy. Disclose when you want something specific that disclosure unlocks, like an adjustment. Do not disclose as an explanation for work that is late, because it lands as an excuse even when it is not one.


This is general information, not legal or medical advice. Employment rights around disclosure vary a great deal by country, so check the position where you are.

People usually ask this at the worst possible moment, which is just after something has gone wrong and they are looking for a way to explain it.

That is the moment to wait.

## What is the question actually for?

Separate two different things people mean by telling someone.

**Asking for something.** An adjustment, a different deadline structure, written instructions instead of verbal, a quieter place to work. This has a purpose and a result.

**Explaining yourself.** Saying it to account for something that already went wrong. This has no mechanism attached and tends to be heard as a reason you will keep going wrong.

The first is usually worth doing. The second rarely is.

## Should I tell my manager?

Depends on the manager and what you want from it.

Worth doing when you have a specific adjustment in mind and a manager who has handled other people's situations reasonably. In that case, lead with the request rather than the diagnosis. Something like: I work much better with written briefs than verbal ones, could we do that. Many useful adjustments do not require disclosing anything at all.

Worth more caution when you have no specific ask, or when your workplace has a record of treating these things badly. Disclosure cannot be undone.

Worth raising with occupational health instead, where your employer has one, if you want an assessment without it going straight to your line manager. How much they pass on varies, so ask before you say anything.

## Should I tell clients?

Usually not, and this is more clear cut.

Clients buy an outcome. They are not your employer, they have no duty to accommodate you, and the information gives them something to worry about without giving them anything useful.

The exception is a long-standing client relationship where you are proposing a change in how you work together. Even then, the change is the subject, not the diagnosis.

| | Tell | Do not tell |
|---|---|---|
| Manager, with a specific adjustment in mind | Often worth it | |
| Manager, after something went wrong | | Wait |
| Occupational health, for an assessment | Often worth it | |
| A client, to explain a late delivery | | No |
| A client, as context for a new way of working | Sometimes | |
| A close colleague you trust | Your call | |

## What do I say instead when work is late?

Something short, without the diagnosis. The thing, the new date, no long explanation.

Long explanations make the other person responsible for managing your feelings about it, which is why they tend to go badly. Most clients want a date, not a reason.

## What if I want people to understand me better?

Reasonable, and worth separating from the work question. Telling your partner, close friends or a trusted colleague is a different decision with different stakes, and for most people it is worth doing.

What it will not do is change how people interpret missed deadlines at work. That is changed by the deadlines, not by the explanation.

## FAQ

**Can my employer discriminate against me if I tell them?**
In many countries ADHD can qualify as a disability with legal protections, and the details vary widely. Check the law where you are before deciding.

**Do I have to tell my employer?**
Generally no, unless there is a specific safety requirement in your role. Again, this varies by country and profession.

**What adjustments are worth asking for?**
Written instructions, clear deadlines rather than open-ended tasks, regular short check-ins, a quieter workspace, flexibility over when the work happens.

**Will telling them explain my performance?**
It explains the mechanism. It does not change the expectations, and people often expect the same output afterwards.
