# How do I stop apologising for being late?

> **Quick answer:** Stop trying to apologise better and start arriving earlier by changing the mechanism. Work backwards from the arrival time rather than forwards from when you leave, build in the step you always forget, and set the alarm for leaving rather than for the event.


Chronic lateness is one of the most socially expensive parts of ADHD, because it reads as not caring about other people's time. For most of the people we work with, the opposite is true, and the apologising has become its own problem.

## Why does lateness happen when you left on time?

Because the plan was built on the best possible version of the journey.

Most people estimate from the last time it went well. No traffic, nothing forgotten, no queue. That version has happened once and gets used as the baseline every time.

There is also the invisible middle. Getting ready is not one task. It is eleven small ones, and the estimate usually covers two of them.

And the deadline problem: leaving at a time is not a deadline with any force until it is already too late. Ten minutes before, there is no pressure. Two minutes before, there is too much.

## What actually changes it

**Work backwards, out loud, in writing.** Not "leave at 2". Arrive at 2.30, so be at the station at 2.10, so leave at 1.55, so stop what I am doing at 1.40. The step people skip is stopping, and that is where the time goes.

**Set the alarm for stopping, not for leaving.** Fifteen minutes before you leave. The transition out of what you are doing is the part that takes the time.

**Add the forgotten step.** Everyone has one. Keys, charging the car, filling the water bottle, the thing you always remember at the door. Put it in the plan by name.

**Plan to be early and bring something.** Arriving fifteen minutes early is only unpleasant if you have nothing to do. With a book or your phone it is fine, and it removes the whole problem.

**Use the real journey time, not your best one.** Look at what it actually took last three times.

| | What we do | What works |
|---|---|---|
| The plan | Leave at a time | Work backwards from arrival |
| The alarm | For the event | For stopping what you are doing |
| The estimate | The journey when it went well | The journey as it usually goes |
| Buffer | None, or five minutes | Fifteen, with something to do |
| The forgotten step | Remembered at the door | Written into the plan |

## What about the apologising?

Shorten it, and let the change do the arguing.

A long apology makes the other person manage your feelings about it, which is more work for them than the lateness was. It also gets less believable each time, because they have heard it.

One line, no explanation, then be on time twice. Two on-time arrivals change how someone describes you more than any amount of explaining.

## Should I tell people it is ADHD?

Usually not as a reason for a specific instance, because in the moment it lands as an excuse.

With people close to you, as general context, often helpful. With a manager, only if attached to something specific you are changing. The useful version is the change, not the label.

## FAQ

**Why am I late even for things I want to do?**
Because the mechanism is the same regardless of motivation. Wanting to be somewhere does not help with stopping, estimating or transitioning.

**Is time blindness real?**
Difficulty estimating and tracking elapsed time is well described in ADHD. Whatever the label, the practical answer is to externalise it with written plans and alarms rather than relying on your sense of it.

**Should I set my clocks fast?**
It works briefly and then you adjust for it. Working backwards from arrival time lasts longer.

**What if I am late because something genuinely ran over?**
Then say so early rather than arriving late with the explanation. A message sent at the moment you know is treated completely differently.
