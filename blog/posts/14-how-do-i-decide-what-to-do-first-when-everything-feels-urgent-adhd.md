# How do I decide what to do first when everything feels urgent?

> **Quick answer:** Everything feels urgent because ADHD flattens priority, so a reply to a message and a contract deadline arrive with the same weight. The fix is not a better ranking method. It is deciding in advance, at a calm moment, so that the decision is not made under pressure by the thing shouting loudest.


Most prioritisation advice assumes you can see the difference in importance between two tasks and are simply choosing badly. That is not usually what is happening.

What is happening is that nine things are presenting as equally loud, and the one that wins is whichever is newest or most uncomfortable to ignore.

## Why does everything feel the same size?

Because ADHD responds to urgency, novelty and emotional weight rather than to importance.

A message that arrived two minutes ago is new, so it feels urgent. A contract due in three weeks is not new, so it feels distant, right up until it is not. The actual stakes do not enter into it.

This is also why the loudest tasks are rarely the valuable ones. Other people's requests arrive with built-in urgency. Your own work does not.

## Why doesn't the usual advice work?

Eisenhower matrices, ABC ranking, scoring systems. These all ask you to make a judgement at the moment of choosing, which is the moment you are least able to make one.

They also take time, which means on a pressured morning you skip them, which is exactly when you needed them.

The useful version is the same decision, made earlier.

## What actually works

**Decide weekly, not daily.** In your Sunday Reset, pick three things for the week. Not twenty-one. Three. Everything else is secondary by definition, which saves you from ranking it later.

**Decide tomorrow's first task tonight.** One task, written down, with its Step Zero. Then the morning contains no decision at all.

**Use one question instead of a matrix.** When something new arrives and feels urgent: does this change if I do it at 4pm instead? Almost everything does not. That single question moves most of the noise out of your Golden Window.

**Give the loud things a slot.** Messages, email, requests. One or two fixed windows a day, outside your best hours. Then they stop competing with your own work, because they have somewhere to go.

| | What usually happens | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Choosing | At 9am, under pressure, from nine options | Last night, from three |
| What wins | Newest or most uncomfortable | Decided in advance |
| Other people's requests | Interrupt the best hours | Get a fixed slot later in the day |
| The ranking method | A matrix you skip when busy | One question, four words |

## What about genuine emergencies?

They exist and they are rarer than they feel. A useful habit: when something presents as an emergency, note whether it actually was, after the fact.

Most people find that two or three things a month are real emergencies, and the rest were someone else's planning problem arriving with urgency attached.

## Why does this keep slipping?

Because the system only runs if something makes it run. Deciding three things on Sunday works when someone asks you on Friday what happened to them.

Without that, week four is the one where the reset gets skipped, and by week six the list is back at twenty-two.

## FAQ

**What if my manager decides my priorities?**
Then your weekly three come from them, and the method is the same. The value is in having three rather than nine.

**How do I say no to things that are not my three?**
Usually you are not saying no, you are saying later. A specific later, like Thursday afternoon, is accepted far more often than people expect.

**What if all three are genuinely urgent?**
Then do them in the order of which causes the most damage if it is late, and tell whoever is affected by the other two.

**Is it bad to work on something that is not on the list?**
Only if it happens most days. Then the list is wrong, which is what the weekly reset is for.
