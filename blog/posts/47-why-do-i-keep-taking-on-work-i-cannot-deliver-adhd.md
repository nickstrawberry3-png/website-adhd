# Why do I keep taking on work I cannot deliver?

> **Quick answer:** Because saying yes happens at the exciting stage and delivering happens at the boring one, and the version of you agreeing is not the version doing the work. Add a delay before you answer, quote from your real past timings rather than your optimistic ones, and count what is already open before you add to it.


The pattern is familiar. An interesting enquiry arrives, you are enthusiastic, you say yes immediately and agree to a date. Weeks later you are behind on three things and wondering what you were thinking.

You were not thinking badly. You were thinking at the point of maximum novelty, which is the worst possible moment to estimate anything.

## Why does the yes feel so easy?

Because at the moment of agreeing, every attractive part of the job is present and none of the boring parts are. A new client, a new problem, a new idea. The edit, the admin and the finishing are abstract and months away.

That is the same mechanism that makes a new project beat an existing one. You are comparing the best part of the new thing against the current reality of everything else.

There is also often a fear of turning work down, particularly for anyone who has had a quiet period. Saying no feels like it might end the flow of enquiries, so you say yes and deal with it later.

## What to change

**Put a delay between the enquiry and the answer.** Twenty-four hours, as a rule, for anything with a deadline attached. Reply immediately to say you are interested and will confirm tomorrow. The enthusiasm fades enough overnight for a realistic answer.

**Quote from your actual history, not your estimate.** Look at how long the last three similar jobs really took, start to delivery. Most people in this pattern quote the time the work would take if nothing went wrong, which has never happened.

**Count what is open first.** Before agreeing to anything, write down everything currently unfinished. If the list surprises you, that is the answer.

**Cap the number of open jobs.** Three is usually right. A new one replaces an existing one rather than joining it.

**Price the realistic timeline, not the optimistic one.** A longer quoted lead time loses fewer jobs than people fear, and loses far fewer clients than a missed delivery.

| | What happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| When you answer | Immediately, while excited | After 24 hours |
| How you estimate | How long it should take | How long the last three actually took |
| What you compare it to | Nothing | Everything currently open |
| Number of jobs | However many arrive | A cap, one in one out |
| Lead time quoted | Optimistic | Realistic, with margin |

## What if I genuinely need the money?

Then this matters more, not less, because the pattern is costing you money rather than making it.

Taking a fourth job you cannot deliver does not produce income, it produces a late delivery, a worse reputation and often a refund. The jobs you have already agreed to are the ones with money attached. Finishing those is the fastest route to being paid.

## How do I say no without losing the client?

Usually you are not saying no, you are offering a later date. Something like: I would like to do this, the earliest I can start properly is the 20th, does that work. A lot of clients will wait, and the ones who cannot were going to be a problem anyway.

For work you genuinely cannot take, referring someone else keeps the relationship and often brings the next enquiry back to you.

## FAQ

**Is it bad to be enthusiastic about new work?**
No, it is an advantage in sales. The problem is only the timing of the commitment, which is why the delay works.

**What if I am scared of a quiet period?**
Common, and the pattern makes quiet periods more likely, because the late deliveries are what stop referrals.

**How do I know my real timings?**
Track start and delivery dates on the next five jobs. Most people find the gap between their estimate and reality is large and consistent.

**Should I tell clients I have a waiting list?**
If it is true, yes. A realistic lead time communicated upfront reads as being in demand rather than as being slow.
