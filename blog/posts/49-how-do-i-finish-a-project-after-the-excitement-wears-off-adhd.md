# How do I finish a project after the excitement wears off?

> **Quick answer:** Treat the last twenty percent as a different task from the rest, because it is. It has no novelty left, no obvious next step and no visible progress. Define what done means in writing while you are still excited, give the finishing work its own slot, and put a person at the end of it.


The shape of this is always the same. The first week is excellent. The middle is fine. And then somewhere around eighty percent the whole thing goes quiet and sits there, sometimes for months.

People assume they lost interest. What actually happened is that the part of the project that was supplying the interest ran out, and the part that is left supplies none.

## Why does the last part feel so different?

| | The first eighty percent | The last twenty |
|---|---|---|
| Novelty | High, everything is new | None, it is all familiar |
| Progress | Visible, large steps | Invisible, small corrections |
| Next step | Obvious | Unclear, lots of small things |
| Feeling | Building something | Tidying something |
| Reward | Frequent | Only at delivery, and often faint |

Every column moves the wrong way at once. So the project does not stall because you stopped caring. It stalls because the fuel it was running on is no longer in it.

## What to do about it

**Write down what done means at the start.** Four or five specific, checkable items. Do this in the first week, while you are excited, because it is the only time you will. Without it the project has no end condition, and a task with no end is one you cannot finish by definition.

**Schedule the finishing work as its own thing.** Not more of the same project. A separate slot, named as finishing, with its own time. Treating it as a continuation is why it never gets a turn.

**Put a person at the end.** A delivery date given to someone real, with something due. Not a date in your own plan. This is the single biggest change and it works because the last twenty percent has nothing else pulling it.

**Make the final items boring and listed.** Write the remaining work out as a checklist of small specific actions. The vagueness of nearly finished is a large part of why it stops.

**Accept the standard you set, not the one you can now see.** By the end you can see every flaw. The client cannot. Deliver against the written definition of done, not against what you can now imagine.

## Why does finishing feel like nothing?

Worth naming, because it is part of the cycle. Many people with ADHD get very little reward from completing something, so the brain learns that finishing costs a lot and pays nothing, while starting pays immediately.

That is why the next new idea is so attractive at exactly the eighty percent mark.

Two things help. Keep a Proof List of finished work where you can see it, because finished work otherwise disappears entirely. And tell someone when you deliver, because the reward your brain does not generate can be supplied externally.

## What if I have several projects stuck at eighty percent?

Finish one. Not all of them, one. Pick the one closest to done, not the most important, and get it out.

Then decide honestly about the rest. Some should be killed in writing rather than left to rot, and killing a project deliberately is a legitimate outcome that frees up far more than it costs.

## FAQ

**Is it normal to lose interest at the end?**
With ADHD, extremely. It is the most common point of abandonment and it is predictable enough to plan for.

**Should I force myself through it?**
Forcing works occasionally and costs more each time. Attaching a person to the deadline works better and is repeatable.

**How do I stop starting something new instead?**
Cap your open projects at three and make a new one replace an existing one. Write new ideas down without acting on them.

**What if the project genuinely is not good?**
Then finish it anyway if someone is paying, and kill it in writing if nobody is. Half-finished is the worst of both.
