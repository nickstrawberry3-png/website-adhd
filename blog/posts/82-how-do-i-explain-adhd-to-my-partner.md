# How do I explain ADHD to my partner?

> **Quick answer:** Explain the mechanism rather than the label, and attach one change they will actually notice. What lands is not the diagnosis, it is why you can do your job and not the dishes, and what you are doing about it. An explanation with no change attached stops working within weeks.


This conversation usually goes badly for a predictable reason. The person explaining is trying to be understood, and the person listening is trying to work out whether anything is going to be different.

Both are reasonable. They just need different things from the conversation.

## What is actually hard to explain?

The inconsistency. You hold down a demanding job and cannot open a letter that has been on the table for three weeks. From outside, that looks like choosing not to.

The explanation that works is not about effort. It is about what starts a task.

Work supplies deadlines, colleagues and consequences you can feel today. Home supplies none of those. The same brain performs completely differently in the two places, and the difference is the structure around it, not how much you care.

A sentence that lands: at work there is always someone waiting, and at home there is nobody, and that turns out to matter more than wanting to.

## What not to do

**Do not lead with the diagnosis as an explanation for a specific grievance.** If the conversation starts the day after something went wrong, it is heard as an excuse, however accurate it is.

**Do not explain at length.** Long explanations put the other person in the position of managing your feelings about it, which is more work for them than the original problem.

**Do not promise it will be different without saying how.** That promise has usually been made before.

## What to do instead

**Pick a calm moment.** Not during or straight after a problem.

**Explain one mechanism, not the whole condition.** The structure point above is usually the most useful one.

**Attach one specific change.** A fixed slot for the thing you avoid, a shared list, a weekly point where you both look at what needs doing. One, not five.

**Ask what actually bothers them most.** Often it is not the thing you assumed. Many partners mind the uncertainty more than the task, and a simple "I will do it Thursday" solves most of it.

| | What usually happens | What works better |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Right after a row | A calm, unrelated moment |
| Content | The whole diagnosis | One mechanism |
| Length | Long | Short |
| Attached change | None, or a promise | One specific thing, with a time |
| Their part | Listening | Asked what bothers them most |

## What if they do not believe in ADHD?

It happens, and arguing about the diagnosis rarely goes anywhere.

Drop the label and talk about the pattern instead. I cannot start things nobody is waiting for, so I am putting a time on them and telling you when, is a complete and workable conversation that requires nobody to agree about anything medical.

The changes will do more to convince them than the explanation would have.

## FAQ

**Should they come to an appointment with me?**
If you are being assessed, some clinicians find a partner's observations useful. Ask first.

**How do I stop them nagging me?**
Usually by replacing nagging with an agreed system, like a fixed time and a shared list. Most partners nag because nothing else has worked, not because they want to.

**What if I have already explained it several times?**
Then stop explaining and change one thing. Repeated explanation without change is what has worn it out.

**Is it fair to ask them to help with accountability?**
A little, and be careful. A partner who becomes your manager usually resents it. Better to get accountability elsewhere and ask them for something small and specific.
