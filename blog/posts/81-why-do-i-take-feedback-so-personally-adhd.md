# Why do I take feedback so personally?

> **Quick answer:** Because the reaction arrives before any thinking does. Rejection sensitivity is widely described in ADHD, and it converts a comment about one piece of work into a verdict about you. Knowing it is disproportionate does not stop it. Having a plan for the first hour does.


This is general information, not medical advice. If this is affecting you badly or persistently, your doctor or a therapist is the right place to take it.

The giveaway is the speed. Someone says something mild, and the physical response is already happening before you have considered whether they had a point.

## Why is the reaction so fast?

Because it does not go through the reasoning part first. It arrives as a body state, and the thinking catches up afterwards, usually to agree with it.

That is why being told it is out of proportion changes nothing. Most people with this know it is out of proportion while it is happening. The knowledge arrives too late to be useful.

## Why does one comment become a verdict?

Because it generalises. A specific remark about a specific piece of work becomes evidence about your competence, then about your character.

A business owner we work with described blaming herself when her own staff made mistakes, on the grounds that she must have set the process up badly. That is the same mechanism with a wider reach.

The practical cost is not the feeling. It is what the feeling makes you do: avoid the person, avoid the work connected to them, then fall behind, which generates real criticism later.

## What to do in the first hour

The first hour decides whether this costs a morning or a week.

1. **Name it.** This is the reaction, not an assessment. Naming it does not remove it and it changes what you do next.
2. **Do not act for an hour.** No reply, no apology, no explanatory message, no asking whether they are annoyed. Most of the damage people do to themselves happens here.
3. **Move.** Ten minutes of walking. Body state shifts faster through movement than through thinking.
4. **Write what was actually said.** Their words, not your interpretation. The gap between the two is usually large and seeing it written down shrinks it.
5. **Finish one small thing.** The reaction says you are failing. A completed task is the only effective contradiction.

## What if the feedback was fair?

It often is, partly, and the reaction makes that harder to use.

Separate the two. Write the specific point, then write the one thing you would do differently. That converts a verdict into a task, and a task is much easier to carry.

| | What the reaction does | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | One comment becomes a verdict on you | Write the actual words down |
| Timing | Act immediately | Wait an hour |
| Response | Apologise at length, or avoid | One line, then keep working |
| Use | Nothing learned, just damage | One specific change, written |

## FAQ

**Is rejection sensitive dysphoria a real diagnosis?**
It is not in the formal diagnostic manuals. It is widely described clinically in people with ADHD and the experience is real regardless of the label.

**Does medication help?**
Some people report it does, some do not. A question for your prescriber rather than for an article.

**How do I ask for feedback without this happening?**
Ask for it in writing and read it later. Written feedback read an hour after it arrives is far easier to use than the same words in a meeting.

**Should I tell my manager I react this way?**
Depends on the manager. A more useful version is asking for feedback in writing, which you can request without explaining why.
