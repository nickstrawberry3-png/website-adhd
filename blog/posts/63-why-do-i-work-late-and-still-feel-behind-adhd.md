# Why do I work late and still feel behind?

> **Quick answer:** Because the hours are going to other people's work and your own is still waiting at six o'clock. Long days with ADHD usually mean a day of interruptions and context switching, not a day of output. More hours cannot fix a problem caused by when the hours are spent.


You are at your desk for eleven hours. You are not slacking. And the three things that actually mattered are still untouched when you leave.

One client described being tired of not having weekends and evenings, while never feeling she was on top of anything. The hours were not producing the outcome they were supposed to buy.

## Where do the hours actually go?

Four places, none of which appear on your list.

**Other people's requests.** They arrive with urgency attached and yours does not, so they win, repeatedly, all day.

**Context switching.** Every interruption costs more than the interruption itself, because returning to the thread is the expensive part. A day of small interruptions can consume most of the available attention without removing much time.

**Deciding.** Each unstarted task gets picked up and put down several times.

**The avoided task.** The one big thing you did not do generates a background cost all day, which is why a day spent avoiding something is more tiring than a day doing it.

## Why does staying late not fix it?

Because by six your capacity is gone, and the work still waiting is the hardest work you have. So the extra hours get spent on email and tidying, which feels like work and produces very little.

Then you go home late, sleep less, and start the next day with less, which makes the next day worse. That is the loop, and more hours feed it.

## What changes it

**Protect one block in your Golden Window.** Two hours, before the day takes them, for the thing that actually matters. Marked as busy. This single change does more than any amount of staying late.

**Give other people's requests a slot.** Two fixed windows for messages and email, outside your best hours. They stop competing with your own work because they have somewhere to go.

**Three things for the week, not twenty-one.** Decided at a weekly review. Everything else is secondary by definition.

**Set a stop time and keep it.** Counterintuitive, and it works. A hard stop forces the prioritising that an open-ended day never requires.

| | What the long day looks like | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| First hours | Email and requests | One protected block on your own work |
| Requests | Answered as they arrive | Two fixed slots |
| What you are working from | A list of twenty-one | Three for the week |
| End of day | Whenever it tails off | A fixed stop |
| Output | Busy, nothing finished | Fewer hours, the right ones |

## What if my workload genuinely is too large?

Sometimes it is, and the three-things exercise is how you find out. If, after protecting your best hours and slotting the noise, the core work still does not fit, that is a resourcing conversation and worth having with evidence.

Walking into that conversation with a written record of where the time goes is considerably more effective than saying you are overloaded.

## FAQ

**Is working late ever the answer?**
Occasionally, for a genuine deadline. As a pattern it produces less work, not more, because it degrades the following days.

**Why do I get more done in the last hour before a holiday?**
Because the deadline is real and proximate. That is the mechanism to replicate with smaller pressure points, not with longer days.

**Should I come in early instead?**
Often better, because your capacity is higher and nobody is asking you for things yet. Worth testing for a fortnight.

**How do I say no to requests?**
Usually you are saying later, with a specific time. Thursday afternoon is accepted far more often than people expect.
