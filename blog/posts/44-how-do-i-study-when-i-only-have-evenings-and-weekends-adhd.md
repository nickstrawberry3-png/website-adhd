# How do I study when I only have evenings and weekends?

> **Quick answer:** Protect the evenings with a small fixed block and treat weekends as a bonus rather than the plan. A plan that depends on six hours on Sunday breaks the first time the weekend goes wrong, and weekends go wrong often. Small and daily survives a full-time job. Large and occasional does not.


Everyone in this position makes the same plan. Not much during the week because work is busy, then a proper session on Saturday and Sunday.

It is a reasonable plan for someone whose motivation is constant. It fails for most people with ADHD, because the weekend arrives, the week has used everything up, and the six hours become none.

## Why do evenings feel impossible?

Because the capacity the study needs has already been spent at work. This is not an excuse, it is the actual mechanism, and it is why the honest answer is to lower the target rather than raise the effort.

There is also a transition problem. Getting home means stopping one mode and starting another, and transitions cost more with ADHD than the tasks on either side of them.

## What works on a full-time schedule

**Twenty to thirty minutes, every working day, fixed time.** Attached to something that already happens so there is no decision. Straight after dinner, or before dinner, whichever you will actually keep.

**Set the floor low enough to survive a bad day.** Ten questions. Fifteen minutes. The floor is the thing that keeps the run intact, and the run is what produces the result over four months.

**Do not go home first if you can avoid it.** For a lot of people, stopping somewhere on the way home works far better than getting home and trying to start again. Once you sit down at home the transition cost doubles.

**Decide tomorrow's topic tonight.** Thirty seconds. Removes the hardest moment from the session.

**Make the weekend a bonus, not the plan.** If it happens, excellent. If it does not, the week still worked. This single change is what stops the whole plan collapsing.

| | Usual plan | What works |
|---|---|---|
| Weekday | Nothing, too tired | 20 to 30 minutes, fixed |
| Weekend | Six hours, the main volume | A bonus on top |
| If the weekend is lost | The week was a write-off | The week still worked |
| Where | At home, after getting in | Somewhere on the way home |
| Target | Ambitious | A floor you can always hit |

## What about the weekend when it does happen?

Treat it as two or three short blocks with real breaks, not one long session. A six-hour block on Saturday exists only in plans.

Use it for the things that genuinely need a longer run: a timed past paper, a topic that needs assembling rather than reviewing. Save the question banks for weekday evenings, because they break into small pieces well.

## How do I protect the evening block from everything else?

Tell the people you live with what it is and when. Most of the interruptions are avoidable and most people will work around a specific time if they know it exists.

And put the phone in another room. The evening block is the most vulnerable session of the day, because resistance is cheapest to give in to when you are already tired.

## FAQ

**Is studying tired worth it at all?**
A short active session while tired is worth considerably more than nothing, and considerably more than a long passive one. Keep it short and keep it retrieval based.

**Should I study before work instead?**
It works well for some people, because capacity is higher. It only works if you will actually do it, so test it for a week before committing.

**What if I work shifts rather than nine to five?**
Anchor the block to the shift rather than the clock, and lower the floor on night shifts rather than skipping.

**How long before the exam should I increase it?**
Add a second short block in the final six to eight weeks rather than lengthening the first. The first one is already at your attention limit.
