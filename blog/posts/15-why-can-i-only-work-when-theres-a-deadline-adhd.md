# Why can I only work when there's a deadline?

> **Quick answer:** Because a deadline supplies urgency, and urgency is one of the few things that reliably starts an ADHD brain. It genuinely works, which is why you keep relying on it. The problem is that it is the most expensive trigger available, and there are four cheaper ones. Finding which one works for a given task is the Spark Check.


A client put it well: deadlines, she hates them, but they are her friend. Another said that without a pressure point she does not even check the list half the time.

Both are describing the same thing. The work is possible. It just needs something outside them to make it start.

## Why do deadlines work when intentions don't?

Because an ADHD brain does not run on importance. It runs on a small set of triggers, and urgency is the strongest of them.

A deadline converts a task from important to urgent, which moves it from the category that never starts into the category that does. Nothing about the task changed. Only the pressure around it.

The reason this feels like a character flaw is that it looks like you only work when forced. What is actually true is that you work when a trigger is present, and the only trigger you have been using is the one that hurts.

## What are the other triggers?

Four of them, and they are what the Spark Check runs through.

**Urgency.** A real deadline with a real consequence. Powerful, expensive, causes the crash.

**Novelty.** Something new in the task. A new tool, a new location, a different order, a different format. Cheap and surprisingly effective on repetitive work.

**Interest.** The part of the task you actually like. Starting there rather than at the logical beginning gets you in the door.

**Pressure from a person.** Someone expecting it, by a stated time. This is the strongest of the four after urgency and by far the cheapest, because it does not require anything to go wrong.

## How do I run a Spark Check?

Take the task you are avoiding and go through the four in order, asking which one you could attach.

| Trigger | Question to ask | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Person | Who could I tell I am doing this, by when? | Message a friend: sending the invoices by 2pm |
| Interest | Which part of this do I not mind? | Start the report with the chart, not the summary |
| Novelty | What could I change about how I do it? | Do it in a café, on paper, in a different order |
| Urgency | Can I set a real deadline earlier than the real one? | Tell the client Wednesday when it is due Friday |

Start with person. It is the one most people have never tried deliberately, and it is the one that fixes the underlying problem, which is that nobody is checking on you.

## Why is "someone expecting it" so much better than a deadline?

Because it produces the pull without the pile-up.

A deadline three weeks away does nothing for twenty days and then does everything in one. A person expecting something on Thursday produces a small, usable amount of pull every few days.

That is also why it does not cause the crash. You never had to override anything.

## What if I live alone and work alone?

Then this is the thing to solve first, not last. It is the most common situation among the people we work with and the one they most often treat as unfixable.

Options that work: a weekly call with another self-employed person, a group where you state what you will do, a client you give interim dates to rather than one final date, or a coach. The form matters less than it being a real person who notices.

## FAQ

**Is it bad to work to deadlines?**
No. Deadlines work. Relying on one enormous deadline with nothing in between is what causes the crash. Several small ones do the same job for less.

**What if I set my own deadline and ignore it?**
Self-set deadlines with no one watching are not deadlines. That is why the person trigger comes first.

**Does this mean I need accountability rather than discipline?**
For most people we work with, yes. Discipline is what you have been using and it has been expensive.

**Can novelty really start a task?**
On repetitive work, often. Changing where and how you do something is a small intervention with a large effect, and it costs nothing to test.
