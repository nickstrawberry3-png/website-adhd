# How do I study for a professional exam with ADHD while working full time?

> **Quick answer:** Short daily blocks beat long weekend sessions, because the weekend plan depends on willpower you will not have and the daily block becomes automatic. Fix one time, make it small enough to do after a bad shift, and tell one person. The exam is months away, so you need a pull that is not the exam.


This is the hardest shape of task for an ADHD brain. Self-directed, repetitive, no immediate consequence, and a deadline far enough away to produce no pull at all until it produces panic.

We see it constantly: USMLE resits, postgraduate entrance exams, CFA, ACCA, nursing certifications, specialty exams. Capable people, working full time, who cannot make themselves open the book.

## Why does the usual revision plan fail?

Because it is built for a different brain.

The standard plan is a timetable covering every topic between now and the exam, usually written in one enthusiastic evening. It assumes consistent motivation across four months, which is exactly the thing you do not have.

It also front-loads the planning, which is itself a satisfying task, and then collapses in week two, at which point the plan becomes evidence of failure and gets abandoned entirely.

## What works instead

**One fixed daily block, small.** Twenty to forty minutes, at the same time, every working day. Small enough that you can do it after a bad shift. Consistency beats volume by a large margin over four months.

**Decide the content the night before.** One topic, written down. Not choose something tomorrow. The decision is the part that fails.

**Active recall, not reading.** Reading feels productive and produces almost nothing. Questions, flashcards, writing from memory. It is less pleasant, which is the point, and it is the difference between studying for hours and remembering nothing.

**Tell someone and report to them.** The exam is too far away to pull you. A person expecting a message every evening is close enough.

**Weekends are for catching up, not for the main volume.** Plan them as a bonus. A plan that depends on six hours on Sunday breaks the first time you work a weekend.

| | Usual plan | What works |
|---|---|---|
| Unit | Three hours on a day off | Thirty minutes daily |
| Decided | In a timetable months ahead | The night before, one topic |
| Method | Reading and highlighting | Questions and recall |
| Pull | The exam | A person, today |
| If you miss a day | Plan is broken, abandon it | Do the next one |

## What about a resit?

Harder, because the attempt carries a record now. Opening the material means remembering failing, so avoidance has an extra layer on it.

Two things help. Start with the section you did best in, not the worst, because the first session needs to prove the work is possible rather than confirm the dread. And get the feedback or breakdown if one exists, so you are working on specifics rather than on everything.

## How do I fit it around shifts?

Anchor to the shift rather than the clock. Thirty minutes before the shift, or immediately after getting home, whichever is more consistent for your rota. Trying to hold the same clock time across a rotating rota does not work.

On night shifts, lower the floor rather than skipping. Ten minutes of questions keeps the thread. Nothing breaks it.

## FAQ

**How many hours a day should I study with ADHD?**
Fewer than you think, more often than you think. Consistent daily blocks beat occasional long ones for retention and for actually happening.

**Why do I study for hours and remember nothing?**
Usually because the method is passive. Reading and highlighting feel like work and produce very little recall. Testing yourself is harder and works.

**Should I ask for extra time in the exam?**
Worth asking about, and the paperwork usually takes longer than people expect, so start early. The process varies by exam board.

**What if I have already lost two months?**
Start today with the small daily block. A consistent six weeks beats a panicked three, and the panicked version is the one that has already failed.
