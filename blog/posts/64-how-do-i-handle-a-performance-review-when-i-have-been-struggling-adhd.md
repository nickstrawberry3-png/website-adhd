# How do I handle a performance review when I have been struggling?

> **Quick answer:** Go in with your own evidence and a plan already underway. A review where you bring the problem, the cause and the change reads completely differently from one where your manager raises it. Write down what you have actually delivered first, because you will have badly underestimated it.


A review after a difficult period is one of the most dreaded conversations there is, partly because you have already had a much harsher version of it in your head.

That internal version is usually missing most of the evidence.

## Start by writing down what you did

Before anything else, list what you delivered this period. Everything, including small things.

This matters because unfinished work is visible and finished work disappears. The inbox shows unread, the list shows undone, and nothing anywhere displays what you completed. So your sense of the period is built almost entirely from the backlog.

People are consistently surprised by this list. Do it before you prepare anything else, because it changes what you walk in believing.

## Then prepare three things

**What went well, with specifics.** Not a feeling. Named deliverables with dates and outcomes.

**What did not, named honestly and narrowly.** One or two specific things, not a general confession. Documentation was consistently late is a problem with a fix. I have been struggling is not.

**What you have already changed.** This is the one that matters most. A change already in place and running for a few weeks is evidence. A change you intend to make is a promise, and promises are what they expect.

| | What makes it go badly | What to bring |
|---|---|---|
| Who raises the problem | Your manager | You |
| How it is described | Vaguely, as struggling | Narrowly, as a specific gap |
| The fix | Will try harder | Already in place, with dates |
| Your delivery record | From memory, underestimated | Written list, specific |
| Tone | Apologising | Reporting |

## Should I mention ADHD?

Only if there is a specific adjustment attached. Naming a diagnosis in a review, with no ask, tends to be received as an explanation for why the problem will continue.

If you want an adjustment, the better route is usually occupational health before the review, so what arrives at the review is a recommendation rather than a disclosure. That takes time, which is a reason to start before you are in trouble.

## What if the review goes badly anyway?

Ask for specifics and a timeframe, in writing. Vague dissatisfaction is impossible to act on and tends to grow. A named list of what needs to be different by when is something you can actually work with, and most managers will give it if asked directly.

Then get one person checking on you, inside or outside work. The gap between knowing what to fix and doing it is the thing that put you here, and it does not close on its own.

## FAQ

**Should I apologise?**
Once, briefly, if something genuinely went wrong. Repeated apologising takes over the conversation and leaves no room for the plan.

**What if I disagree with the feedback?**
Ask for the specific examples behind it. Disagreeing in general goes badly, disagreeing with a specific example you can address goes fine.

**Should I bring up workload?**
If it is a real factor, with evidence of where the time goes. Without evidence it sounds like a deflection.

**What if I am put on a formal process?**
Take it seriously, get the criteria in writing, and get support, including from a union or professional body if you have one. These processes are survivable and much more so with a documented plan.
