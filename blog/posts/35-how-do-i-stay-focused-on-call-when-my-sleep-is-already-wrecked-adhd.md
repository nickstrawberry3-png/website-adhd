# How do I stay focused on call when my sleep is already wrecked?

> **Quick answer:** You cannot think your way through sleep deprivation, so the aim is to reduce what you are asking of your attention rather than increase your effort. Externalise everything onto paper, do the judgement work early, use checklists for anything repetitive, and protect the one sleep block you can actually get.


This is general information, not medical advice. If you are regularly unsafe through fatigue, that is a conversation for occupational health and your own doctor, and it is a reasonable one to start.

Being on call with ADHD means working at reduced capacity on exactly the functions that were already stretched: working memory, prioritising, and switching between tasks without losing the thread.

## What actually goes first when you are tired?

Not clinical knowledge. That holds up longer than people expect.

What goes is holding several things at once. The three jobs you were about to do, the result you were waiting for, the thing the nurse told you in the corridor. With ADHD that capacity is limited on a good day. On four hours of sleep it is not there at all.

So the strategy is not to focus harder. It is to stop relying on memory.

## What to do instead

**Write everything down, immediately, on paper.** Not in your head, not on your phone. One list, one page, in your pocket. Every job goes on it the moment you hear it, before you do anything else.

**Keep the list visible.** The value is not in having written it, it is in seeing it. A list in a pocket you never open is the same as no list.

**Do the judgement work early.** The decisions that need thought go in the first part of the shift. The mechanical work can survive the hours when you are worst.

**Use a checklist for anything repeated.** Not because you do not know the steps. Because knowing the steps and performing them while exhausted are different things.

**Say the handover out loud and write it.** Verbal handover alone is the single easiest thing to lose when tired.

| | What fails when tired | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Holding jobs in your head | First thing to go | One paper list, written immediately |
| Remembering a corridor request | Gone within minutes | Write it before you walk away |
| Deciding what is urgent | Flattens, everything feels equal | Rank once, early, on the list |
| Repeating a known process | Steps get skipped | Checklist, even for familiar things |
| Handover | Details drop | Written and spoken |

## What about the sleep itself?

Take whatever block you can get rather than chasing a perfect one. Twenty minutes is worth having. A protected two hours is worth a great deal.

The thing worth avoiding is late caffeine to push through the last stretch, because it buys the end of this shift at the cost of the next one.

If you are driving home after nights, treat that as part of the shift rather than after it. Falling asleep at the wheel is the most common serious harm from this, and it is worth planning for rather than hoping.

## When is this not a personal problem?

If you are consistently working beyond safe fatigue limits, that is a rota problem, not a focus problem, and no amount of personal strategy fixes it. Raise it formally. Clinicians are unusually reluctant to do this, and the reluctance does not make the rota safer.

## FAQ

**Does ADHD make fatigue worse?**
It affects the same functions that sleep deprivation affects, so the two stack. The practical effect is that the same shift costs more.

**Should I take medication to get through a night shift?**
Not a decision to make from an article. Timing and shift work is a known question and belongs with your prescriber.

**Why do I forget things people tell me in corridors?**
Because corridor requests arrive with no prompt and go straight into working memory, which is the weakest point. Write them down before you continue walking.

**Is it worth telling the team I am struggling on nights?**
Often yes, with one person you trust. A colleague who knows to put things in writing rather than say them in passing makes a real difference.
