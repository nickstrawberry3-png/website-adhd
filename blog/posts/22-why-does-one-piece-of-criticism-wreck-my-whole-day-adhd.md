# Why does one piece of criticism wreck my whole day?

> **Quick answer:** Because rejection sensitivity is common with ADHD, and the reaction is immediate and physical rather than reasoned. Knowing it is disproportionate does not stop it. What helps is a plan you run in the first hour, before the reaction has finished making decisions for you.


This is general information, not medical advice. If this is affecting you badly or persistently, your own doctor or a therapist is the right place to go.

A one-line comment from a manager. A client who replies more briefly than usual. Someone who does not reply at all. And the rest of the day is gone.

The part people find hardest to explain is that they know, while it is happening, that the reaction is too big. Knowing changes nothing.

## What is rejection sensitivity?

A strong, fast emotional reaction to real or perceived criticism, rejection or disapproval. It is widely described in ADHD, though it is not a formal diagnosis in its own right.

The important feature is speed. It arrives before any thinking happens, which is why reasoning with it after the fact does not work. By the time the reasonable part of you is involved, the reaction has already set the tone of the day.

## Why does it cost a whole day?

Because it does not stay an emotion. It turns into behaviour.

A VP we work with described handling it well externally, asking directly whether something was wrong, then spending the rest of the day monitoring and adjusting around that person. The external response was fine. The internal cost was most of her remaining energy.

A business owner described blaming herself whenever anything went wrong, including things her staff did, on the grounds that she must not have set the process up properly.

Both are doing the same thing: converting one comment into hours of background work.

## What is the Burnout Loop Breaker?

A short routine for the first hour after it happens, because the first hour is where the day is decided.

1. **Name it.** Say to yourself that this is the rejection reaction, not an assessment of the facts. Naming it does not remove it and does change what you do next.
2. **Do not act for one hour.** No reply, no apology, no explanatory message, no asking someone whether they are annoyed. Most of the damage people do is in this hour.
3. **Move.** Ten minutes of walking or anything physical. This shifts the body state faster than thinking does.
4. **Write the facts.** What was actually said, in their words. Not what you inferred. The gap between the two is usually large.
5. **Pick one small task and finish it.** This matters more than it sounds. The reaction tells you that you are failing, and a finished thing is the only effective contradiction.

## How do I stop it costing me the week?

The thing that extends it from a day to a week is the second-order reaction: avoiding the person, avoiding the work connected to them, and then falling behind, which produces real criticism later.

So the rule that matters is to keep doing the work. Not to feel fine. Just to keep going, badly if necessary.

## What if the criticism was fair?

It often is, partly. The reaction makes that harder to use, because it turns a specific comment about one piece of work into a verdict on you.

Separating them helps. Write down what was said, then write what you would change next time. One or two lines. That converts it into a task, and tasks are much easier to carry than verdicts.

## FAQ

**Is rejection sensitive dysphoria a real diagnosis?**
It is not in the formal diagnostic manuals. It is widely described clinically in people with ADHD and the experience is real, whatever the label.

**Does medication help?**
Some people report it helps, some do not. Worth raising with your prescriber rather than acting on anything you read here.

**Is this the same as being oversensitive?**
No. The speed and physical intensity are different, and most people with it are highly aware that the reaction is out of proportion, which is not typical of general sensitivity.

**Should I tell people I react this way?**
With people close to you, often helpful. In a workplace, depends entirely on the workplace.
