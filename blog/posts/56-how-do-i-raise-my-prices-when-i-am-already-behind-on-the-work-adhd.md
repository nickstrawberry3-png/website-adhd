# How do I raise my prices when I am already behind on the work?

> **Quick answer:** Being behind is usually the argument for raising prices, not against. Fewer jobs at a higher price produces the same income with less to deliver, which is the most direct fix available. Raise it for new enquiries only, write the number down before the conversation, and say it without explaining.


People in this position usually have the logic backwards. They feel they cannot raise prices until they have caught up, got organised and started delivering reliably.

So the price stays low, the volume stays high, the backlog stays, and the condition for raising the price never arrives.

## Why does raising prices help a backlog?

Because a backlog is usually a volume problem wearing a delivery problem's clothes.

If you are behind on twelve jobs at one price, you would be on time with seven at a higher one, for the same money. That is not a trick. It is the only lever most service businesses have that works immediately.

It also changes the kind of client you get. Higher prices tend to bring people who are clearer about what they want and less likely to generate the small chaotic work that eats time.

| | Stay cheap and catch up | Raise prices |
|---|---|---|
| Volume needed | High | Lower |
| Delivery pressure | Constant | Manageable |
| Income | Same | Same or higher |
| Backlog | Persists | Shrinks |
| Client type | More, smaller, more chaotic | Fewer, clearer |

## What about the clients I already have?

Leave them. You do not need to renegotiate existing work, and trying to is usually what stops people raising prices at all.

New enquiries only. Then the existing clients move to the new price at their next project, or they do not, and you have stopped depending on them either way.

One client raised her prices and her clients accepted it. That is the common outcome and it is almost always less dramatic than the version people imagine.

## How do I actually say it?

**Write the number down before the conversation.** Do not calculate it live. Live calculation under mild social pressure produces a lower number almost every time.

**Say the price and stop talking.** Most discounting happens in the sentence after the number, which exists only to fill the silence.

**Do not justify it.** You are not explaining a rise, you are quoting a price. Justification invites negotiation.

**Expect to lose some work.** Losing a proportion of enquiries on price is normal and healthy. Winning every job you quote for means you are too cheap.

## What if I feel I do not deserve it yet?

Common, and worth separating from the business question.

The feeling usually comes from the backlog, which is exactly what the higher price fixes. Waiting to feel ready is the same mechanism as waiting to get organised before hiring: a condition that never arrives.

Price on what the work is worth to the client, not on how you currently feel about your reliability.

## FAQ

**How much should I raise it by?**
Enough to notice. A small rise produces the same awkward conversation for a much smaller result.

**What if I lose clients?**
Some will go. The question is whether total income and delivery pressure improve, and usually both do.

**Should I warn existing clients?**
Only if you are moving them. For new enquiries there is nothing to warn anyone about.

**When is the right time?**
At the next enquiry. Waiting for a better moment is the thing that has already cost you a year.
