# Why do I miss out on work I actually want?

> **Quick answer:** Because the work goes to whoever replies, and the reply is the thing you cannot start. Enquiries that sit unopened for a week are enquiries someone else has already answered. This is usually the most expensive single consequence of ADHD in a freelance business, and it is also the most fixable.


A videographer we worked with was approached by a football club and a restaurant in the same period. He wanted both. He answered neither, because opening the message meant facing everything else he had not done.

He was the only person doing that work in his town. The work still went elsewhere.

## Why does this happen when you want the work?

Because wanting it is not what makes you reply. Replying needs a specific action at a specific moment, and the moment the enquiry arrives is usually the moment you are in the middle of something else, or avoiding something else.

Then the delay compounds. A reply on day one is a reply. A reply on day eight needs an explanation. So the task gets heavier while the opportunity gets colder.

There is also the pile effect. If your inbox already contains things you have not dealt with, a new enquiry does not arrive alone. It arrives attached to all of them, and the whole pile is what you are declining to open.

## What is this actually costing?

Worth working out in a number, because most people have never done it and the number tends to be larger than expected.

Count the enquiries you did not answer in the last six months. Multiply by your average job value. Multiply by a conservative conversion rate, say one in three.

That figure is what the unopened inbox is costing per six months. For most freelancers we work with it is considerably more than any coaching, software or assistant would cost.

## What to change

**Reply within 24 hours, always, even when the answer is no.** The speed matters more than the content. A fast no keeps you in the running for the next enquiry. A slow yes often arrives too late.

**Have a holding reply ready.** Two lines: thanks, interested, I will confirm by tomorrow. Send it the moment anything arrives. It buys you a day and it stops the enquiry going cold.

**Check enquiries in a fixed slot, separate from everything else.** Twice a day, five minutes, looking only for new enquiries. Not your whole inbox, because your whole inbox is the thing you are avoiding.

**Separate the enquiry channel from the pile.** A filter, a label, a different address. New opportunities should not arrive into the same place as the things you feel guilty about.

| | What happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| New enquiry arrives | Into the pile you are avoiding | Into its own filtered place |
| When you see it | Days later | A fixed five-minute check, twice a day |
| First response | A full considered reply, eventually | Two-line holding reply within the hour |
| If you cannot take it | No reply | A fast no |
| The old pile | Blocks everything new | Dealt with separately |

## What about the opportunities I have already lost?

Some are recoverable. A message three months later saying you are now available is worth sending, and people are usually more receptive than you expect.

Pick the three best and send one line each. Do not try to recover all of them, and do not include a long apology.

## FAQ

**Is it worth replying if I am already full?**
Yes. A fast no with a date you could start, or a referral, keeps the relationship. Silence ends it.

**Should I use an auto-responder?**
It buys a little time and does not replace a real reply. Useful alongside the holding message, not instead of it.

**What if I do not know whether I can take the job?**
Say exactly that, with a date you will confirm by. Uncertainty communicated is fine. Silence is not.

**Should I hire someone to handle enquiries?**
Often worth it once the lost-work number is clear, and the number is usually what makes the decision obvious.
