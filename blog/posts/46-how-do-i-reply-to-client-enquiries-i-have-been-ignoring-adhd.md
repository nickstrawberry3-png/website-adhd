# How do I reply to client enquiries I have been ignoring?

> **Quick answer:** Open them all in one pass without replying to anything, then send two-line replies from a template. Most of the paralysis is in composing an apology from scratch for each one. Write the apology once and reuse it, and reply before you feel ready, because the silence is costing you more than the delay.


A freelancer we spoke to had five people who wanted to hire him and had not replied to any of them. He lived in a town of fifty thousand where he was the only person doing his kind of work. He described seeing the messages arrive and being unable to open them.

He was turning down work he wanted, by doing nothing.

## Why is opening a message so hard?

Because opening it creates an obligation that did not formally exist a second earlier. Unread, it is a message. Read, it is a reply you owe.

So the unread count functions as a way of keeping the workload down, which it does not, but it feels that way in the moment.

Then there is the compounding problem. A message unanswered for two days needs a short reply. At three weeks it needs an apology, and the apology is now the thing you are avoiding rather than the reply itself. Every day makes the task bigger without you doing anything.

## The method

**One: open everything without replying.** Sit down and read all of them in one pass. No answers, no decisions. You will almost always find they are smaller than the dread suggested, and some will have resolved themselves.

**Two: write the template once.** Three lines. Something like: thanks for getting in touch and sorry for the slow reply, here is where I am, and here is what happens next. Save it. The composing is where the paralysis lives, and you only need to do it once.

**Three: reply shortest first.** The easiest message first, not the most important. The purpose of the first reply is to prove the task is possible.

**Four: send before it is good.** A two-line reply today beats a considered one never sent. Most enquiries want to know whether you are available, not to read prose.

**Five: give a date you can keep.** Under-promise. You are rebuilding trust with someone who has waited, and a second missed date is much worse than a slow first reply.

| | What we do | What works |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | One at a time, when ready | All in one pass, no replies |
| The apology | Rewritten each time, agonised over | One template, reused |
| Order | Most important first | Shortest first |
| Length | Long and careful, unsent | Two lines, sent |
| The date offered | Optimistic | Realistic, with room |

## What about the ones from months ago?

Reply anyway. The version in your head where they are furious is almost never the version that exists.

Keep it short, do not explain at length, and say whether you are available now. Some will have moved on, which is fine and is information. Some will not have, and that is work you had written off.

Do these in a separate session from the current ones, so the dread of the old pile does not stop you dealing with this week's.

## How do I stop it happening again?

A fixed slot, two or three times a week, outside your best hours, and one person who knows it exists. Notifications have already been tried and they do not work, because the problem is not awareness.

The other change worth making is replying to new enquiries within a day even when you cannot take the work. A quick no costs nothing and keeps you in the running for the next one.

## FAQ

**What do I say about why I did not reply?**
As little as possible. One line, no explanation. Long apologies make the message harder to write and harder to read.

**Should I admit I was overwhelmed?**
With a long-standing client, sometimes. With a new enquiry, no. They are buying an outcome.

**What if I have hundreds of unread emails?**
Deal with the last two weeks properly and archive the rest. Anything important comes back.

**How do I know what to charge after a long gap?**
Your current rate. The delay is not a reason to discount, and discounting to apologise sets up the next problem.
