# How do I get through an inbox I have been avoiding for weeks?

> **Quick answer:** Do not try to clear it. Deal with the last two weeks properly and archive everything older, because anything genuinely important in there will come back. Then open the recent ones in one pass without replying, and send two-line answers from a template. The volume is not the obstacle, the dread of the pile is.


Four thousand unread. Or four hundred, or forty. The number matters less than the fact that opening the inbox now produces a feeling rather than a task.

Every plan you have made to clear it has involved a free afternoon, and the free afternoon has not happened.

## Why does the inbox get like this?

Because every email read is an email you now owe something on. Unread, it is information. Read, it is a task.

So the unread count works as a way of keeping the workload down. It does not, but it feels that way in the moment, and that feeling is what builds the pile.

Then the pile develops its own weight. Opening the inbox means seeing three weeks of things you did not do, which is unpleasant, so you open it less, so it grows. That loop is the actual problem.

## The method

**One: archive everything older than two weeks.** All of it, in one action, without reading. This is the part people resist and it is the part that works. Anything important will be re-sent, chased or raised in a meeting. Nothing of consequence lives only in a four-week-old email.

**Two: open the remainder in one pass, replying to nothing.** Read them all. Decisions later. You will find most need nothing and the real ones are fewer than the dread suggested.

**Three: reply shortest first, two lines each.** A short answer today beats a considered one never sent. Most senders want information, not prose.

**Four: write the late template once.** One apology line, the answer, a next step. Reuse it. Composing the apology from scratch each time is where the paralysis lives.

**Five: fixed slot, twice a day, from now on.** Not continuously, not when you feel ready. Two fixed windows, outside your best hours.

| | What we do | What works |
|---|---|---|
| The old pile | Plan to read it all | Archive it unread |
| Opening | One at a time, when ready | All recent ones in one pass |
| Replies | Long, careful, unsent | Two lines, sent |
| The apology | Rewritten each time | One template |
| Ongoing | Checked constantly or not at all | Two fixed slots a day |

## Is archiving unread really safe?

In almost every job, yes. Test it if you do not believe it: archive, and note over the next month how many things you genuinely lost. Most people find the answer is none.

The exceptions are jobs with formal record-keeping or compliance obligations, where you archive rather than delete and the content stays searchable. That is the point of archiving rather than deleting.

## What about the things I genuinely did not do?

Some of them matter. Deal with those as a separate session, not mixed into the inbox work.

List them, pick the one with the largest consequence, send one line today with a date attached. The silence around them is doing more damage than the delay, and that is true even several weeks in.

## FAQ

**Should I use folders and filters?**
A small number, yes. One filter for anything needing action and one for anything from your manager is worth more than an elaborate structure you stop maintaining.

**What about inbox zero?**
It works for some people and it is not the goal here. The goal is that nothing important is lost and opening the inbox is not unpleasant.

**Why do I read an email and then not reply?**
Because reading is cheap and replying is a task with no deadline attached. Reply in the same action or do not open it yet.

**Should I tell people I am behind?**
With anyone waiting on something specific, yes, with a date. Generally, no.
