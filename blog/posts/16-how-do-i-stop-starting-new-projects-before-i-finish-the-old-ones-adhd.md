# How do I stop starting new projects before I finish the old ones?

> **Quick answer:** Because a new project is the most rewarding thing available: it is novel, it has an obvious first step, and it has no history of failure attached. The old project has none of those. The fix is to make finishing visible and to cap how many things can be open at once.


A software developer we spoke to was running four projects and finishing none. A publisher had more ideas than he could count and no completed one. A data analyst described the pattern as hyperfocusing, putting in enormous effort, then dropping it, over and over.

None of them had an idea shortage. All of them had the same problem at the same point in the cycle.

## Why does the new project always win?

Because of when the switch happens. It is almost never at the start of a project and almost never at the end. It is at the middle, where the novelty has gone and the finishing work begins.

At that exact moment, a new idea arrives carrying everything the current project has lost.

| Current project at the middle | A new idea |
|---|---|
| Familiar | Novel |
| Next step is unclear and dull | First step is obvious and exciting |
| Carries a record of being late | No history at all |
| Finishing is the boring part | Starting is the fun part |

So it is not a fair contest, and losing it is not a discipline failure. It is the predictable outcome of comparing two things on the one axis an ADHD brain uses.

## What actually stops it?

**Cap the number of open projects.** Three is usually the right number. Not three a day, three in existence. Everything else goes on a list you do not look at. The cap is what makes the next rule possible.

**One in, one out.** A new project can start, and it has to replace an existing one, which means either finishing it or formally killing it. Killing it is allowed and it is much better than letting it rot half-done.

**Name the finishing work.** Most people have never written down what finishing actually means for a project. Do it at the start, while you are excited, because that is the only time you will. Four or five bullet points of what "done" is.

**Keep a Proof List.** The things you have finished, written as they happen, where you can see them. People in this pattern have a very distorted sense of their own output, because unfinished work is visible and finished work disappears.

## What about the ideas I keep having?

Write them down and do not act on them. An idea captured loses most of its urgency within about a week, and the ones that survive that week are the ones worth looking at.

This is the same mechanism as the Later Pad applied at project scale. The thought and the action are two separate events, and the capture is what separates them.

Keep the list. Do not open it daily.

## Why is finishing so unsatisfying?

Worth naming, because it is part of why the cycle continues. Many people with ADHD get very little reward from completing something. One client said there was no relief at the end, nothing.

If completion does not pay, the brain learns that finishing is a cost with no return, and starting is where the reward lives. The Proof List is a partial fix, because it makes the finished work visible instead of letting it vanish. Telling someone when you finish works better still.

## FAQ

**Is having lots of ideas a bad thing?**
No, it is a genuine advantage and most of our clients have it. The problem is the ratio of started to finished, not the number of ideas.

**What if the new project really is better?**
It might be. Then kill the old one properly and say so, rather than leaving it open. Abandoning deliberately is fine. Abandoning silently is what builds the pile.

**How do I finish something I have lost interest in?**
Run a Spark Check on the finishing work specifically. Usually the answer is a person expecting it by a stated date.

**What if I have twelve half-finished projects already?**
Pick one, finish it, kill the rest in writing. Do not try to rescue them all. The rescue plan is another new project.
