# How do I pick one idea and stay with it?

> **Quick answer:** Pick on the basis of which one you could get a first customer for soonest, not which one is most interesting, because interest will go either way. Then commit for a fixed period rather than forever, write the others down, and get one person who asks about the chosen one weekly.


People in this position usually frame it as indecision. It is rarely that. They can tell you about all five ideas in detail and argue for each.

The difficulty is that choosing one means losing the other four, and each of those still has all its novelty intact.

## Why is choosing so hard?

**Every unchosen idea is still perfect.** It has no problems yet, because you have not worked on it. The chosen one acquires problems immediately, which makes the others look better by the day.

**Choosing means committing to the boring parts.** Picking an idea means accepting its admin, its finishing work, its dull middle. Keeping all five means never reaching any of that.

**Researching options is itself a satisfying task.** Comparing ideas is novel, clear and produces visible output. It can absorb months.

## How to actually choose

**Use time to first customer, not interest.** Which of these could have one person paying soonest. Interest is not a useful criterion, because it will fade on whichever you pick, and it does not predict which will work.

**Commit for a fixed period, not forever.** Ninety days on this one. Forever is an unmakeable decision, ninety days is not, and ninety days of real work tells you more than another year of comparing.

**Write the others down properly and close the file.** One page each so the thinking is not lost. Then do not open it. The list exists so your brain stops holding them.

**Tell someone which one you picked.** This matters more than it sounds. An unannounced decision is easy to quietly reverse.

| | What usually happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Basis for choosing | Which is most exciting | Which reaches a paying customer soonest |
| Commitment | Forever, so never made | Ninety days |
| The other ideas | Kept live, competing | Written up once, closed |
| Who knows | Nobody | One person, told |
| What counts as progress | Refining the plan | One real customer |

## What if I pick wrong?

Ninety days of real work on the wrong idea is more useful than nine months of comparing, because you will have learned something specific rather than something hypothetical.

And you will know. Running an idea properly for a quarter produces real information about whether it works, which no amount of thinking about it produces.

## What if the boredom hits in week six?

It will. That is the point at which the other ideas start looking excellent again, and it is predictable enough to plan for.

Decide now what you will do about it. The usual answer is a person expecting something weekly, and a written reminder of why you chose this one, written today while you still believe it.

## FAQ

**How many ideas should I keep on the list?**
As many as you like, as long as you are not opening it. The list is storage, not a menu.

**What if two ideas could be combined?**
Usually they cannot, and combining is often a way of avoiding the choice. Pick one, build it, add the other later if it still makes sense.

**Should I validate before committing?**
A week of talking to potential customers, yes. A three-month research project, no, that is the comparing problem in a different coat.

**What if I have already started three?**
Finish or kill two. Not pause, kill, in writing, so they stop taking up attention.
