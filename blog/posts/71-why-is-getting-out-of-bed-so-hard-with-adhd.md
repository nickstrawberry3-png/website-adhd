# Why is getting out of bed so hard with ADHD?

> **Quick answer:** Because getting up is a transition with no immediate reward, made at the moment of lowest capacity, usually after too little sleep and with the phone within reach. It is not laziness. The fix is a fixed wake time, light early, and deciding the first action the night before so the morning contains no decision.


This is general information, not medical advice. Persistent difficulty getting up is worth mentioning to your own doctor, since several treatable things look like this.

Everyone finds mornings harder some days. What people describe here is different: lying awake, fully conscious, unable to make the move from horizontal to vertical, sometimes for an hour.

## Why is it so much harder than it looks?

**It is a transition, and transitions are the expensive part.** Stopping one state and starting another costs more with ADHD than either state does. Getting up is a pure transition with nothing else attached.

**There is no reward in it.** The next thing is usually a routine you have done a thousand times. Nothing novel, nothing urgent, nobody waiting.

**You are at your lowest capacity.** Whatever resource starting requires is at its minimum when you wake.

**The phone is there.** Picking it up requires no decision, and forty minutes later the situation is worse.

**Sleep timing is often delayed.** Many people with ADHD do not feel sleepy until late, so the alarm arrives in the middle of their biological night rather than at the end of it.

## What actually helps

**Fix the wake time, not the bedtime.** The same time every day, including weekends, for two weeks. Bedtime follows wake time. Trying to go to bed earlier when you are not tired produces an hour of lying awake.

**Get light within an hour of waking.** Daylight if possible, a bright lamp if not. This is the strongest lever available over the body clock and it costs nothing.

**Decide the first action the night before.** One specific thing, written down. Not get up and be productive. Put the kettle on. The morning should contain no decision.

**Put the phone across the room.** It handles the alarm and removes the forty minutes.

**Give the morning one thing to turn up for.** Coffee you like, a shower, something with a small reward in it. A morning with nothing in it is harder to enter.

| | What we do | What works |
|---|---|---|
| Alarm | Snoozed, phone in reach | Across the room, no snooze |
| Wake time | Varies, later at weekends | Same every day for two weeks |
| First action | Decided while lying there | Written down last night |
| Light | Curtains closed | Daylight within the hour |
| Bedtime | Pushed earlier, unsuccessfully | Follows the fixed wake time |

## What if I have tried all of this?

Then it is worth a conversation with your doctor, because several things that are not ADHD produce exactly this.

Worth mentioning: sleeping a full night and waking unrefreshed, heavy snoring or pauses in breathing, restless legs in the evening, or this starting or worsening when a medication changed. Sleep apnoea, restless legs, thyroid problems and low iron all cause mornings like this, as does perimenopause. All of them are worth investigating and most have straightforward treatments.

## FAQ

**Is this sleep inertia or ADHD?**
Sleep inertia is normal and brief. If you are fully awake and still cannot move for a long period, that is closer to the transition problem described here.

**Does medication help with mornings?**
It cannot help before you have taken it, which is the obvious problem. If the gap between waking and taking it is the thing wrecking your mornings, that is worth raising with your prescriber rather than solving on your own.

**Is a sunrise alarm worth it?**
Many people find it helps, and the evidence for light timing generally is good. Cheap enough to test.

**What if my schedule changes constantly?**
Anchor what you can. Shift workers get less from a fixed wake time and more from controlling light exposure and protecting the longest sleep block.
