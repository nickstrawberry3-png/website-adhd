# How do I manage a team when I have ADHD?

> **Quick answer:** Put the structure in writing rather than carrying it in your head, run short regular check-ins instead of long infrequent ones, and let your team chase you. The parts of management that are hard with ADHD, follow-up, consistency and remembering what you asked for, are all solvable by making them external.


Managing people adds a layer that most ADHD strategies do not cover. It is no longer only your own work that depends on your follow-through.

The good news is that a team is a structure, and structure is what you have been short of. Used properly it makes things easier rather than harder.

## What is actually hard about managing with ADHD?

**Follow-up.** You ask for something, and three weeks later nobody has checked. The asking is easy and the remembering is not.

**Consistency.** Intense attention for a fortnight, then nothing for a month, which the team experiences as unpredictability.

**Holding several threads.** What you asked each person, what is outstanding, what you promised to come back on.

**Self-blame when something goes wrong.** One owner told us she blames herself for anything her staff get wrong, on the grounds that she must not have set the process up properly. That is exhausting and it also stops problems getting fixed, because the response is guilt rather than a change to the process.

## What works

**Everything in writing, in one place they can see.** Not your notebook. A shared list of who is doing what and by when. If it only exists in your head, it will go.

**Short and regular beats long and occasional.** Fifteen minutes weekly with each person is worth more than an hour a month, because the gap is where things get lost. It also gives you a fixed point to prepare for, rather than a vague sense that you should check in.

**Let them chase you.** Tell your team explicitly that if they are waiting on you, they should ask again. Most people will not do this unless given permission, and your approvals are the most common bottleneck.

**Decide once, write it down.** Verbal decisions get forgotten and then reversed, which is the single most destabilising thing for a team. Writing it down protects them from your inconsistency.

**Protect your Golden Window from management.** Meetings and approvals in the afternoon, your own work in the morning. Otherwise the team absorbs all your best hours and your own work never happens.

| | Where it goes wrong | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Follow-up | Remembered, then forgotten | A shared written list |
| Check-ins | Long, infrequent, cancelled | Fifteen minutes, weekly, fixed |
| Bottleneck | Waiting on your approval | They have permission to chase |
| Decisions | Verbal, later reversed | Written at the time |
| Your own work | Whatever is left | Protected hours before meetings |

## Should I tell my team?

Depends on the team and what you want from it. Telling them in order to explain a pattern rarely helps. Telling one trusted person so they put things in writing rather than saying them in passing often does.

What matters more than disclosure is the changes being visible. A manager whose decisions are written down and whose check-ins happen is a good manager, whatever the reason for the system.

## FAQ

**Am I a bad manager because of this?**
Not inherently. The common strengths, crisis handling, idea generation, reading people, are real. The weak points are follow-through and consistency, and both are fixable with structure.

**What if I keep being the bottleneck?**
Reduce what needs your approval. A lot of it does not, and deciding that once removes the recurring problem.

**Should I use project management software?**
If the team will use it. The shared written list matters, the specific tool does not.

**How do I stop blaming myself when something goes wrong?**
Turn it into a process question. What would have caught this. That is both more useful and less expensive than the guilt.
