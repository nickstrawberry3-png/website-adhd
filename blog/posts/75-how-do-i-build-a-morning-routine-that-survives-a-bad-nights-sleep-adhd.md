# How do I build a morning routine that survives a bad night's sleep?

> **Quick answer:** Make it three things, not ten, and define a shorter version you will still do on the worst morning. Most morning routines fail because they were designed for a good day and offer no option on a bad one. The floor is the part that makes it a routine rather than an occasional good morning.


Everyone has built one of these. Wake at six, water, journal, meditate, exercise, read, plan the day. It lasts eleven days.

Then one bad night arrives, the whole thing is skipped, and skipping it once turns into not having a routine at all.

## Why do morning routines collapse?

**Too many parts.** Each one has a cost, and six habits at once means paying six costs on a morning when you have very little.

**No short version.** A routine that is all or nothing becomes nothing the first time the morning is compromised.

**Designed by the enthusiastic version of you.** Written on a good evening, to be executed by someone who slept badly and has a difficult day ahead.

**Optimised rather than tested.** Most routines are assembled from advice rather than from what actually works for the person.

## What a routine that survives looks like

**Three things, maximum.** For most people: a fixed wake time, light, and one specific first action. That is enough. Everything else is addition.

**A floor version, decided in advance.** What you do on the worst morning. Get up at the fixed time, open the curtains, drink water. Thirty seconds. It still counts and it keeps the thread.

**The first action decided the night before.** One specific thing. Not be productive. Put the kettle on, or open the document.

**No phone until the three things are done.** This is the one that protects all of it, because the phone is what consumes the morning when capacity is low.

| | The routine that fails | The one that holds |
|---|---|---|
| Number of parts | Six to ten | Three |
| On a bad morning | Skipped entirely | A thirty-second floor version |
| Designed by | The optimistic version of you | Tested over two weeks |
| First action | Decided in the moment | Written last night |
| Phone | First thing | After the three things |

## Why is the fixed wake time the non-negotiable one?

Because it determines everything downstream. Sleep timing, energy through the day, when you get hungry, when your focus window falls.

And it is the one that holds the others in place. A routine with a variable start time is not a routine, it is a list of things you sometimes do.

Same time every day including weekends, at least for the first fortnight. After that a shorter lie-in is survivable, but the drift back usually starts there.

## What about after a genuinely terrible night?

Floor version, and do not attempt to make up for it during the day.

The usual response to a bad night is to push harder to compensate, which costs more than it recovers and makes the following night worse. The better response is the floor version in the morning, a lower expectation for the day, and protecting that night's sleep rather than working late to catch up.

## FAQ

**How long until a routine becomes automatic?**
Longer than the figures people quote, and it varies a lot. The floor version is what gets you there, because an unbroken run of small actions beats an interrupted run of ambitious ones.

**Should I meditate or journal in the morning?**
Only if you will. Both are useful and both are commonly the first things dropped. Add them after the three core things are reliable.

**What if my mornings are not mine, because of children or shifts?**
Then the routine fits inside what exists. A fixed wake time and light still apply. The first action might be two minutes.

**Does it have to be early?**
No. Consistent matters, early does not. A reliable eight o'clock beats an aspirational six.
