# How do I price my work when I have no idea how long it will take me?

> **Quick answer:** Stop pricing by your estimate and start pricing by your record. Track the real start-to-delivery time on your last several jobs, use the slowest realistic figure rather than the best, and price in fixed packages rather than hourly so that your variable speed stops being the client's problem or yours.


This question usually arrives attached to a bigger one. People who cannot predict how long work takes end up either underpricing badly or avoiding quoting at all, and both cost real money.

The unpredictability is genuine. A job that takes four hours when you are engaged can take three weeks when you are not, and that is not something you can fix by trying to be more consistent.

## Why are the estimates always wrong?

Because you are estimating the working time and charging for the elapsed time.

The actual hours of work might be six. The job takes a month because of the two weeks before you could start it and the week of avoidance in the middle. Those weeks are real and they belong in the quote.

There is also the hyperfocus distortion. Most people estimate based on the session where everything flowed, because that is the memorable one. It is not the representative one.

## What to do instead

**Track start to delivery, not hours worked.** On your next five jobs, write down the date you agreed it and the date you delivered. That number is what you need, and almost nobody has it.

**Use your slowest realistic figure.** Not your worst ever, not your best. The one that is true most of the time. Then add margin on top of that.

**Price in packages, not hours.** Hourly billing punishes you for being fast and makes every slow week a confession. A fixed price for a defined deliverable removes both problems and is what most clients prefer anyway.

**Define what is included, in writing.** Number of revisions, what counts as a change, when the final version is final. Scope creep is where the hours disappear, and an undefined job has no end condition.

**Charge a deposit.** This matters more than it sounds. A deposit creates an obligation on day one, which supplies the one thing the job otherwise lacks, which is a person waiting.

| | Hourly | Fixed package |
|---|---|---|
| Rewards | Slow work | Finishing |
| What the client sees | Your variable speed | A price and a date |
| When you hyperfocus | You earn less | You earn the same |
| Scope creep | Absorbed in hours | Visible as a change |
| Deposit | Unusual | Normal |

## What about raising prices when I am behind?

A separate problem, and the answer is usually yes, raise them anyway.

Being behind is often a volume problem. Fewer jobs at a higher price produces the same income with less to deliver, which is the most direct fix available to someone in this position.

Raise the price for new enquiries only. You do not need to renegotiate existing work, and trying to is usually what stops people doing it at all.

## How do I quote confidently when I do not feel confident?

Write the number down before the conversation and read it out. Do not calculate it live, because live calculation under mild social pressure produces a lower number almost every time.

Say the price, then stop talking. Most of the discounting people do happens in the sentence after the number.

## FAQ

**Should I tell clients my timelines are unpredictable?**
No. Build the uncertainty into the quoted date instead. They are buying a date, not an explanation.

**What if my quote is too high and I lose the job?**
Losing some jobs on price is normal and healthy. Winning every job you quote for is a sign you are too cheap.

**How many revisions should I include?**
Two is a common and workable number. The point is that it is a number, because unlimited revisions removes the end of the project.

**What if I have never tracked my timings?**
Start now, and until you have the data, take your instinct and add half again. Most people find that is still short.
