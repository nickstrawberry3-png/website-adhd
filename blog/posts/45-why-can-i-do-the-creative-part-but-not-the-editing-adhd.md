# Why can I do the creative part but not the editing?

> **Quick answer:** Because shooting, writing and designing supply novelty and the editing does not. Every edit is the same process repeated, with no new input and no one waiting. The skill gap is not real, the trigger gap is. Editing needs a trigger attached on purpose, because it will never produce one itself.


A videographer we worked with could shoot all day without effort. Weddings, events, commercial work, travelling to places he would never otherwise go. He described all of it as easy.

Editing was impossible. He had a wedding from the previous June still unfinished, four projects waiting, and five clients he had not replied to.

He was not a bad editor. He had been doing it professionally for years.

## Why does the creative part work and the finishing part not?

Compare what the two supply.

| | Shooting, writing, designing | Editing and finishing |
|---|---|---|
| Novelty | Every job is different | Identical process every time |
| Input | New people, new places | Material you have already seen |
| Someone present | Usually yes | No |
| Feedback | Immediate | None until delivery |
| Interest | It is why you do this | None |

The creative part arrives with every trigger an ADHD brain uses. The edit arrives with none, so it loses every time, and no amount of caring about the work changes that.

There is a second layer on creative work specifically. Watching back what you made means judging it, and most people in this position are hard on their own work. The videographer described finishing something and never wanting to look at it again, convinced it could be better. That makes the edit an unpleasant task as well as a boring one.

## What actually helps?

**Split the edit into named stages with different characters.** Rough cut, sound, colour, export. These are genuinely different tasks, and treating them as one undifferentiated block of editing makes the whole thing refuse to start.

**Start with the stage you least mind.** Not the logical first step. If colour is the bit you enjoy, open the project and do colour on one clip. The point is to be inside the project.

**Make Step Zero opening the file.** Open the project. That is a complete session. Untouched and started are very different states, and getting from one to the other is the expensive part.

**Change where you do it.** Editing somewhere other than your usual desk supplies the novelty the task lacks. This sounds trivial and works more often than it should.

**Give the client an interim date.** A rough cut on the 8th. This is the single most effective change, because it inserts a person into a task that otherwise has nobody waiting.

## What about the perfectionism?

It is doing more damage than the boredom in a lot of cases.

If every pass through the edit produces more things to fix, the project has no end condition, and a task with no end condition is one your brain correctly refuses to start.

Write down what done means before you begin. Four or five specific items. When those are met, it is delivered, regardless of what you can still see. Clients almost never see what you see.

## FAQ

**Should I outsource my editing?**
Often worth it, and it usually pays for itself once you count the work you are turning down. Handing it over still needs a first session to set up, so that handover needs its own slot.

**Why do I avoid work I am good at?**
Being good at something does not make it stimulating. Competence and interest are separate, and the trigger problem runs on interest.

**What if I have several unfinished projects?**
Pick the easiest, not the oldest. The first delivery needs to prove the work is possible, and the oldest one carries the most guilt.

**Is it worth changing software?**
Rarely. Learning new software is a satisfying project that replaces editing, and you will arrive at the same wall in the new one.
