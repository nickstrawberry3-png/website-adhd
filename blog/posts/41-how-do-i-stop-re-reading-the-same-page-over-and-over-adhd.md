# How do I stop re-reading the same page over and over?

> **Quick answer:** Re-reading happens because your eyes continue while your attention has already left, and going back feels like the responsible fix. It is not. Stop re-reading, read with a question in mind, and test yourself at the end of each section instead of checking whether you absorbed it.


You reach the bottom of the page and realise you have no idea what it said. So you go back to the top. Three paragraphs in, it happens again. Twenty minutes later you are on the same page, increasingly frustrated.

This is one of the most common experiences people describe, and the instinct to go back is what turns it from a moment into an evening.

## Why does attention leave while the eyes keep going?

Because reading is largely automatic once you are fluent. The mechanical part continues without attention, so there is no signal telling you that you have stopped taking anything in.

With ADHD this gap opens faster and more often, particularly on dense or uninteresting material. It is not that you are not trying. The tell is that it almost never happens on something you find interesting.

## Why doesn't going back fix it?

Because you are re-running the same process that just failed, under worse conditions.

The second pass has less novelty than the first, so attention leaves sooner. The third is worse again. Meanwhile frustration builds, which costs energy, and the page becomes something you dread.

Re-reading is also passive, so even when it works it builds very little memory. You can read a page five times and still not be able to say what is on it.

## What to do instead

**Do not go back.** Keep going to the end of the section even if you have lost it. Then test yourself on the whole section. What you can retrieve is what you got, and that is usually more than it felt like.

**Read with a question in mind.** Turn the heading into a question before you start reading under it. Now you are looking for an answer, which keeps attention engaged in a way that passive reading does not.

**Stop at your Drift Point.** If attention leaves after twelve minutes, read for twelve minutes and stop. Reading past it produces pages you will not remember and will re-read tomorrow.

**Use your finger or a pointer.** Mechanical, and it helps more people than expect it to. It gives the eyes a pace and makes drifting noticeable sooner.

**Read out loud for the hardest parts.** Slower, and much harder to do while absent.

| | What we do | What works |
|---|---|---|
| When attention goes | Go back to the top | Finish the section, then test |
| Before reading | Start at the first word | Turn the heading into a question |
| Session length | Until the chapter is done | Stop at the Drift Point |
| Checking understanding | Re-read and see if it feels familiar | Close the book and retrieve |
| Hard passages | Read again more slowly | Read aloud |

## What if the material is genuinely dense?

Then reading is the wrong first tool. Go to questions on the topic first, get some wrong, and then read. Having a specific gap to fill changes reading from passive scanning into looking something up, which holds attention far better.

## FAQ

**Is this a sign of a reading problem?**
Not usually. If reading has been difficult since childhood in a way that goes beyond attention, that is worth mentioning to your doctor, since dyslexia and ADHD often occur together.

**Does reading on screen make it worse?**
For many people yes, mostly because the device carries other things. Paper or a dedicated reader removes that.

**Should I take notes while reading?**
Short notes from memory at the end of a section, yes. Copying as you go is passive and tends to replace attention rather than support it.

**How do I know if I actually understood it?**
Close the book and say it out loud. If you cannot, you did not, regardless of how familiar it felt.
