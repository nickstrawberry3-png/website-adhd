# What is the best task app for someone with ADHD?

> **Quick answer:** The one you already have. App choice makes almost no difference to whether tasks get done, and switching apps is itself a satisfying project that replaces doing the work. What matters is a weekly point where things come off the list and a person who asks whether you did them.


This is one of the most searched questions in ADHD productivity, and the honest answer is unpopular, because the search itself is part of the problem.

One client had been looking for her perfect setup for two years. She had Notion, with a Top 3 that had grown to 22 items, a Quick Capture she had not opened in months, and a weekly view she ignored. Her question was how she had got this far with no system that worked.

She was successful. She was not short of a system. She was short of the two things no app provides.

## Why doesn't the app matter?

Because every app does the same job: it remembers things. That job is not the one that is failing.

What is failing is some combination of starting, choosing, and nobody noticing whether anything happened. No app addresses any of the three. They can display a task in a nicer typeface and they cannot make you open the file.

So the app you pick changes the surface and leaves the mechanism untouched.

## Why does switching feel so productive?

Because setting up a new system is an almost perfect ADHD task. It is novel, it has a clear first step, it produces visible results quickly, and it feels like working on the problem.

It is also a reliable way to spend a weekend without doing any of the work you were avoiding. Many people have done this five or six times.

| Setting up a new app | The work you are avoiding |
|---|---|
| Novel | Familiar |
| Obvious first step | Unclear first step |
| Immediate visible result | Result much later |
| No history of failure | Carries guilt |

## So what should I actually use?

Whatever you have now. If you have nothing, use paper, because paper does not need opening, charging or logging into.

Then add the three things that do matter.

**A weekly removal point.** Fifteen to thirty minutes, same time each week, where things come off the list. This is the Sunday Reset. Most lists fail from growth, not from the wrong software.

**Three things a week, not a day.** A number you can keep in your head without consulting anything.

**One person who asks.** The piece no app replaces. Someone who knows what your three are and asks on Friday.

## Are there any app features worth having?

A few, and they are minor compared to the above.

Quick capture that works in two seconds, so you can write something down before the thought goes. Something that works offline. Something that does not require a decision about where to file things, because that decision is friction and friction is where it dies.

That rules out the most configurable apps, which is a useful signal. If your system has eleven views, most of them exist for the pleasure of having built them.

## What about the pile of old tasks?

Do not migrate it. This is the single most common way a new system dies in week one.

Start the new list empty, with three things on it. Keep the old list in a file somewhere and look at it in a month. Most of what was on it will have stopped mattering.

## FAQ

**Is Notion bad for ADHD?**
Not bad, but configurable, which means it rewards building over using. If you are spending more time arranging it than working from it, that is the signal.

**Is paper better than digital?**
For many people, yes, because it needs no opening and no decisions. The best test is which one you will actually look at on a bad day.

**Should I use a calendar instead of a task list?**
Putting the three things in the calendar helps, because it forces you to confront how long they take. Keep it simple.

**What about ADHD-specific apps?**
Some are well designed. None of them solve the two things that matter, so judge them on whether they make capture faster, not on their marketing.
