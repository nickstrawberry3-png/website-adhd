# How do doctors and nurses with ADHD keep on top of documentation?

> **Quick answer:** By making the writing part of the clinical encounter rather than something that comes after it, using a fixed skeleton so there is never a blank page, and putting two protected documentation points inside the shift. The people who stay on top of it are not faster writers, they write earlier.


This question comes from people who are good clinicians and know it, and who are also quietly behind on paperwork in a way they do not talk about at work.

Having worked in hospitals in the UK and Australia, I will say plainly that the systems are badly designed for anyone, and worse for an ADHD brain. That is context, not an excuse, because the backlog still lands on you.

## What makes clinical documentation specifically hard?

**It is deferred by design.** The record is written after the thing it describes, so there is always a gap, and the gap is where it goes wrong.

**It has no natural end.** A clinic list ends. Documentation expands to fill whatever is left, so it never produces the sense of being finished.

**It competes with interruptions.** Every bleep, every question, every handover restarts the task, and restarting is the expensive part.

**The consequence is delayed.** Nothing happens today if the entry is thin. It happens months later, or never, or at the worst possible moment.

## The method that works

**One: a fixed skeleton.** Same headings every time, in the same order. Write it once, keep it somewhere you can copy from, or use the system's template if there is a usable one. The point is that you never face an empty box.

**Two: write something in the room.** Three lines is enough. The diagnosis, the plan, the one thing you will forget. Flesh it out later if needed. The expensive part of late documentation is reconstruction, not typing.

**Three: two fixed points per shift.** Mid-shift and before the last hour. Not at the end. Protect them the way you would protect a break, which is to say imperfectly, but deliberately.

**Four: hardest first.** When you do sit down, the complicated one goes first, while you still have capacity. Most people do the easy ones first because they feel productive, and leave the one that matters for the moment they have nothing left.

| | What usually happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| When | After the shift | In the room, then two fixed points |
| Starting point | Blank box | Fixed skeleton |
| Order | Easy ones first | Hardest first |
| Unit | The whole backlog | One entry |
| If interrupted | Start that entry again | Leave the skeleton open, return to it |

## What about the backlog I already have?

Do not try to clear it in one sitting. That plan has already failed, probably more than once.

Pick the oldest clinically significant one and do only that. Then one a day, at a fixed time, until it is gone. Tell one colleague you are doing it, because the reason it has sat there is that nobody is checking.

If the backlog is large enough to be a governance issue, raise it before someone else does. That conversation is much easier when you bring it.

## FAQ

**Can you be a doctor or nurse with ADHD?**
Yes, and plenty are. The clinical work often suits an ADHD brain well. The documentation and administration around it are where the difficulty concentrates.

**Does ADHD medication help with notes?**
It helps many people start, which is a real part of the problem. It does not supply the urgency the task lacks, so the timing changes still matter.

**Should I ask for extra admin time?**
Reasonable to ask, and more likely to be granted if you bring a specific proposal, like protected time mid-shift rather than extra time at the end.

**What if I am already in trouble over documentation?**
Get the facts in front of someone early, with a plan attached. A documented plan you are following reads completely differently from a backlog someone else found.
