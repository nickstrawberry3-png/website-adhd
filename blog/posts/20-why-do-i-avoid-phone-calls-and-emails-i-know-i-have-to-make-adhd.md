# Why do I avoid phone calls and emails I know I have to make?

> **Quick answer:** Because each one is an unknown quantity with no script, and opening it tends to reveal more work rather than finish any. Avoidance makes them worse, because time passing adds an apology to every message. Treat them as a batch with a fixed slot and a template, not as individual decisions.


A videographer told us he could see client messages arriving and could not open them, because opening one meant discovering more he had not done. He was turning down work he wanted, in a town where he was the only person doing that job.

A non-profit VP said she avoids phone calls because she cannot read people as well without seeing them, and dislikes putting others through the same.

Different reasons, identical outcome.

## Why are these specifically so hard?

Four things stack up.

**No script.** Unlike most tasks, you cannot plan the whole thing in advance, because the other person's response is unknown. That uncertainty is enough to stall the start.

**Opening creates work.** An email read is an email you now owe a reply to. So not opening it feels like keeping the workload down, which is why unread counts grow.

**It is a transition.** Picking up the phone means stopping what you are doing and entering a different mode. Transitions cost more with ADHD than the task on either side of them.

**Time adds shame.** A message unanswered for three days needs a short reply. At three weeks it needs an apology, and the apology is what you are now avoiding, not the reply.

That last one is why the pile accelerates.

## How do I actually get through them?

**Batch them into one slot.** Same time, two or three times a week, outside your best hours. The start is the expensive part, so pay it once and do eight, not once per message.

**Write a template for the late ones.** Three lines. Apology, the thing they asked for, a next step. Write it once and reuse it. Most of the paralysis is in composing the apology from scratch every time.

**Set the Step Zero as opening, not replying.** Open all of them and read them in one pass without answering anything. You almost always find they are smaller than the dread suggested.

**Reply short.** A two-line reply sent today beats a considered one never sent. Most senders want the information, not the prose.

**For calls, write the first sentence down.** Not a script, just the opening line. The start is the hard part and having the first sentence removes it.

| | What we do | What works |
|---|---|---|
| When | Whenever we feel ready, so never | A fixed slot, two or three times a week |
| Unit | One message at a time | A batch |
| The apology | Composed from scratch, each time | One template, reused |
| Length | Carefully written, unsent | Two lines, sent |
| Calls | Rehearse the whole conversation | Write the first sentence only |

## What about the really old ones?

The ones from months ago feel unanswerable. They are not, and the reply is shorter than you think.

Something short, honest and without a long explanation works. Most people reply normally. Almost nobody responds the way the version in your head does.

Do these in a slot of their own, not mixed in with current ones, and do the easiest first.

## Why does this keep happening after I clear it?

Because clearing the backlog is a crisis task and you are good at those. When the crisis ends, so does the trigger.

The thing that stops it returning is the fixed slot plus someone who knows it exists. Not a reminder.

## FAQ

**Why is a phone call harder than an email?**
It happens in real time, so there is no editing and no exit. For some people the reverse is true, because a call ends and an email sits there.

**Should I apologise for a late reply?**
One line, no explanation. Long apologies make it about you and are harder to write, which is part of why the message never gets sent.

**What if I have hundreds of unread emails?**
Do not try to clear them. Deal with the last two weeks and archive the rest. Anything important in the archive will come back.

**Is there a way to stop it building up?**
A fixed slot and a person who asks. Notifications and reminders have already been tried.
