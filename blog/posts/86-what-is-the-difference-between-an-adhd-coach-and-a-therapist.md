# What is the difference between an ADHD coach and a therapist?

> **Quick answer:** A therapist is licensed, trained to treat mental health conditions, and works largely on how you feel and why. A coach is unregulated, works on what you do this week, and usually includes contact between sessions. Different jobs, different training, and only one of them is a regulated profession.


This is general information, not medical advice. If you are struggling with your mental health, your own doctor is the right first step.

This post covers what each one actually is. If your question is which one you need right now, that is a different question and we have answered it separately.

## The practical differences

| | Therapist | ADHD coach |
|---|---|---|
| Regulated | Yes, licensed and accountable to a body | No, anyone can use the title |
| Training | Formal, lengthy, supervised | Varies enormously, from substantial to none |
| Can diagnose | Some can, depending on profession | No |
| Can prescribe | No, that is a doctor | No |
| Main focus | Mental health, past experience, patterns of thinking | Energy, systems, starting, finishing, accountability |
| Timeframe discussed | Often the past and present | This week and next |
| Between sessions | Usually nothing | Usually check-ins |
| Typical length | Open-ended or a set course | A fixed programme, often 8 to 16 weeks |
| Confidentiality | Bound by professional codes | Depends entirely on the individual |

The regulation row is the most important one and the least discussed. Therapy is a protected activity with professional accountability behind it. Coaching is not, which means the quality range is enormous and the responsibility for checking sits with you.

## What does a therapy session actually look like?

Broadly, a conversation focused on understanding. What is happening, where it comes from, what patterns are at work, and how to change the way you respond.

For ADHD specifically, some therapists work with the emotional side: the shame that accumulates from years of missed deadlines, rejection sensitivity, anxiety that developed alongside it. Those are therapy problems and coaching does not address them well.

## What does a coaching session actually look like?

Broadly, a working session focused on what you will do.

For ADHD, that usually means looking at the week, finding where things break, and changing something specific. Sleep and energy before systems. What the first action of a task is. Who is going to notice if it does not happen.

The part that does the most work is often not the session. It is the contact in between, because the underlying problem for most people is that nobody is checking on them.

## Can a coach treat ADHD?

No. Coaching is not treatment, and anyone presenting it as treatment is someone to avoid.

A coach also cannot diagnose. If someone offers to tell you whether you have ADHD, that is a clear signal to walk away.

## How do I tell a good coach from a bad one?

Since the field is unregulated, ask directly:

1. What is your background and what qualifies you to work on this
2. What happens between sessions
3. What specifically will be different in twelve weeks
4. Have you worked with people in my situation
5. What would make you tell me I am not a good fit

The last one is the most useful. Anyone with no answer to it will take anybody's money.

## FAQ

**Can I see both at once?**
Yes, and plenty of people do. They work on different layers and do not conflict.

**Is one more expensive?**
It varies by country and by individual. Therapy is often available through public health systems or insurance. Coaching rarely is.

**Does a coach need to have ADHD themselves?**
Not necessarily, and many good ones do. What matters more is whether they understand the mechanism and have worked with people like you.

**Is coaching covered by insurance?**
Usually not, because it is not a clinical treatment. Worth checking your own policy.
