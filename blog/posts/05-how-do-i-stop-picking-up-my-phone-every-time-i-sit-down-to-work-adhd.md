# How do I stop picking up my phone every time I sit down to work?

> **Quick answer:** Blocking apps mostly fail because the phone is still within reach, and reaching for it is automatic rather than chosen. The thing that works is distance. Put the phone in another room during your work block. Everything else is a smaller version of the same idea.


Most people have already tried the obvious. Screen time limits, app blockers, focus modes. They work for about a week.

Then we learn the passcode by heart, or we uninstall the blocker, or we simply open the app in a browser instead.

The reason none of it holds is that we are trying to win an argument with ourselves at the exact moment we are least able to win it.

## Why doesn't willpower work on this?

Because the reach for the phone is not a decision. By the time we notice we are holding it, the choice has already happened.

Our clients describe it the same way again and again. One told us he gives himself fifty minutes of scrolling before starting, which he knows is absurd and does anyway. Another blocked social media during work time, then found herself watching Netflix instead. The blocker moved the behaviour without touching the cause.

The cause is that the phone is nearer than the work, and easier than the work, and always available.

## What is Phone Lockdown?

Three things done before the work block starts, not during it.

**Out of the room.** Not face down, not in a drawer, not in your bag. Another room. Distance is the only part of this that reliably works, because it inserts a physical action between the urge and the phone.

**Notifications off.** Not silent. Off. A phone in another room that buzzes still pulls you out of the room.

**Screen greyscale.** Lower return on this than the first two, but it genuinely reduces the pull of apps built around colour.

## What if I need my phone for work?

Common, and usually overstated. Ask what specifically you need it for. Most answers are either rare, like one client who had to hear a doorbell for arriving staff, or not actually urgent.

Where it is genuinely needed, two options work. Use it for that one function only and leave everything else logged out, or set a specific person who can reach you and silence the rest.

That doorbell client is a good example of taking the constraint seriously. She could not wear headphones because she needed to hear staff arrive, so she never started anything in that window. The fix was not discipline. It was a doorbell she could hear from a different setup, which gave her back ninety minutes a day.

## What about the urge itself?

It still comes. The Later Pad is for that.

A physical pad next to you. When an urge arrives, to check something, to look something up, to message someone, write it down and carry on. Most of them lose all their force once written, and the few that matter are still there afterwards.

| | Usual approach | What works |
|---|---|---|
| Phone position | Face down on the desk | Another room |
| Notifications | On silent | Off |
| The urge | Resist it | Write it down, carry on |
| When set up | During the work block | Before it starts |

## FAQ

**Do app blockers work for ADHD?**
Sometimes, for a few weeks. They fail because the phone is still in reach. Use them alongside distance, not instead of it.

**What if I use my phone as a timer?**
Buy a physical timer. They cost very little and remove the main excuse for keeping the phone nearby.

**Is phone use worse with ADHD?**
Phones supply novelty and instant reward, which is exactly what an ADHD brain is looking for when a task is boring. So the pull is stronger, and it is not a willpower failure.

**How long should a phone-free block be?**
Start at your Drift Point, which is the honest number of minutes before your attention goes. For many people that is fifteen to twenty-five, not an hour.
